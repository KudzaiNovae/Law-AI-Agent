THE STATE

v

TATENDA MUTONHO MATURURE


IN THE HIGH COURT OF ZIMBABWE

DUBE-BANDA J with Assessors Mrs M Chademana and Mr J. G. Nish MASVINGO 24 March 2026; 29 May 2026; & 13 July 2026




Ms Mutumhe, for the State Ms Bwanya, for the accused


DUBE-BANDA J:


  1. The accused is appearing before this court charged with the crime of murder as defined in s 47(1) of the Criminal Law (Codification and Reform) Act [Chapter 9:23] (“the Criminal Law Code”). The allegations are that on 27 June 2025 at Maturure Village, Chief Gutu, the accused, unlawfully caused the death of Torerai Mutonho “the deceased” by stabbing him with a knife once on the chest, intending to kill him or realising that his conduct may cause death and continued to engage in that conduct despite the risk or possibility.

  2. The accused was legally represented throughout the trial. The State tendered an outline of the State case, which is before the court and marked Annexure A. It shall not be necessary to repeat the entire contents of the State outline. It now forms part of the record. The accused tendered into the record an outline of his defence case, which is before the court and marked Annexure B. The accused, whilst admitting stabbing the deceased, pleaded self-defence and implored the court to find him not guilty of the charge of murder and acquit him.

  3. The State produced a post-mortem report compiled by Dr Godfrey Zimbwa at Masvingo Hospital on 30 June 2025. The report is before the court and marked Exhibit 1. Following the examination of the remains of the deceased, the pathologist concluded that the cause of death was haemorrhagic shock from a stab wound. The State produced the accused’s confirmed, warned and cautioned statement recorded by the police on 29 June 2025. The statement was confirmed by a magistrate on 30 June 2025. It is before the court as Exhibit 2.

  4. The prosecutor sought and obtained an admission from the accused in terms of s 314 of the Criminal Procedure and Evidence Act [Chapter 9:07]. This is related to the evidence of Godfrey Zimbwa as contained in the summary of the state case. He is employed by the Ministry

of Health and Childcare as a Medical Officer currently attached to Masvingo Hospital. His qualification is MBchB. He examined the remains of the deceased and recorded a post-mortem report referred to above.

  1. The State called three witnesses, and the accused testified in his own defence. We are going to summarise the evidence briefly. The first to testify was Ngonidzashe Ruocha. The deceased was her husband, and the accused is her nephew. The accused resided at a neighbouring homestead, approximately one hundred metres away. On 27 June 2025, in the evening, at around 1900 hours, the accused returned from a traditional gathering and, while on the road, he was shouting obscenities. When he was about twenty metres from the deceased’s homestead, he continued shouting obscenities; the deceased went out of the kitchen hut to reprimand him against shouting obscenities. The accused stabbed the deceased with a knife. The witness did not see the accused stabbing the deceased; however, the deceased returned to the kitchen hut and said, “I have been stabbed by Tatenda”. He was stabbed on the chest. The accused remained at the homestead holding his knife; he refused an instruction to put it down. He then fled from the homestead. The deceased was bleeding and later died before he could be taken to a medical facility. Under cross-examination, she disputed that there was a fight between the accused and the deceased. She accepted that there was no good relationship between the accused and the deceased because the accused did not like to be reprimanded for his bad behaviour. She disputed the existence of an inheritance dispute between the deceased and the accused.

  2. The second witness to give oral evidence is Oriah Gavaya. The deceased was his brother, and the accused a nephew. When he got to the deceased’s homestead, he found Ngonidzashe Ruocha holding the accused. The accused’s clothes were blood-stained. He saw the deceased, who was bleeding from the chest. He asked the accused to hand over the knife, but he refused. He went to look for a vehicle to take the deceased to the hospital. On his return, the deceased had died. He did not know of any hostile relationship between the accused and the deceased emanating from an inheritance dispute.

  3. The third witness to give oral evidence is Edias Mugabe. He is a police officer and the investigating officer in this matter. The accused voluntarily and without duress gave a statement wherein he admitted stabbing the deceased and throwing the knife into the forest. He said the deceased struck him with a stone. The stone was searched for and was not found. His investigations did not show any hostile relationship between the accused and the deceased.

  4. After the conclusion of the testimony of Edias Mugabe, the prosecution closed its case.

Defence case

  1. The accused elected to give evidence under oath. He testified that he had a long-standing inheritance dispute with the deceased. On 27 June 2025, he went to a neighbouring village to attend a traditional party. On his return, at approximately 2000 hours, he placed his snares to catch mice between his homestead and that of the deceased. After placing the snares, he was struck by a stone on the head. He fell, and when he got up, he saw the deceased, who uttered some words to the effect that the accused would die because of the disputed inheritance. A fight started between the two of them, and he felt that he was being overpowered. He tried to flee, but the deceased held him by the belt. The deceased pulled a knife from the accused's pocket and opened it. The deceased tried to stab him on the chest; he evaded the stab and pushed the deceased, who fell. By the time the deceased fell, he was stabbed in the chest with a knife. He stabbed himself by mistake. The deceased pulled the knife from the chest and threw it to the ground. The accused picked up the knife, the two walked home, and the accused supported the deceased so that he could not fall. In examination in chief and under cross-examination, the accused said he was sorry about stabbing the deceased because the two did not have any problem, and there was no bad blood between them. He did not intentionally stab the deceased.

Legal principles

  1. The State must prove the guilt of the accused beyond a reasonable doubt. An accused person must be acquitted if the State's evidence is not strong enough. He should be acquitted if there exists a reasonable possibility that his version may be true. See S v Alex Carriers (Pvt) Ltd & Anor 1985 (3) SA 79 (T); S v Radebe 1991 (2) SA 166 (T); S v Munyai 1986 (4) SA 712

(V). As stated in S v Schackell 2001 (4) SA 1 (SCA) para 30:

“It is a trite principle that in criminal proceedings the prosecution must be prove its case beyond a reasonable doubt and that a mere preponderance of probabilities is not enough. Equally trite is the observation that in view of this standard of proof in a criminal case, a court does not have to be convinced that every detail of an accused’s version is true. If the accused’s version is reasonably true in substance, the court must decide the matter on the acceptance of that version. Of course, it is permissible to test the accused’s version against the inherent probability, but it cannot be rejected merely because it is improbable; it can be rejected if it can be said to be so improbable that it cannot reasonably be true.” (My emphasis).


  1. When a court is faced with a situation where there is a doubt as to what happened in a particular case, the doubt must be resolved in favour of the accused. In S v Chindunga S-21-02, the court stated that where an accused gives a reasonable explanation of his actions, that explanation cannot be rejected out of hand. See also S v Kuiper 2000 (1) ZLR 113 (S); S v

Manyika 2002 (2) ZLR 103 (H); S v Chabalala 2003 (1) SACR 134 (SCA); S v van Aswegen

2001 (2) SACR 97 (SCA).

  1. It is with these legal principles in mind that this court will proceed to analyse the evidence presented in this trial. In analysing the evidence before us, we start by considering the events that occurred on the fateful day. It is common cause that there is neither direct evidence nor an eyewitness to the stabbing of the deceased. The prosecution relies on circumstantial evidence for the contention that it is the accused who stabbed the deceased. In dealing with each piece of evidence and the inferences to be drawn therefrom, two important principles must be borne in mind. The first is that the court must examine all the evidence. A court does not look at the evidence implicating the accused in isolation to determine whether there is proof beyond a reasonable doubt, and so too it does not look at the accused’s version in isolation to determine whether it is reasonably possible that it might be true. As was stated in R v Hlongwane 1959

    1. a

      (3) SA 337

      t 341A, the correct approach is to consider the totality of the evidence. The second principle, which the court must bear in mind in assessing the evidence, involves the approach to be taken in drawing inferences. Inferences are not to be mere speculation but are to be based on fact. The court in R v Blom 1939 (AD) 188 laid out two rules of logic to be

followed. The first rule is that the inference sought to be drawn must be consistent with all

proven facts. The second rule is that the proven facts must be such that they exclude every other reasonable inference. If the rules of logic are not satisfied, the State would have failed to prove its case, and the accused must be acquitted.

  1. The evidence is that the deceased and his family, while seated in their kitchen hut, heard the accused, who was on the road nearby, hurling obscenities and vulgar words. As the accused got closer to the homestead, the deceased went out to reprimand him. After a very short while, the deceased returned to the kitchen hut bleeding from a stab wound. The deceased told Ngonidzashe Ruocha that “I have been stabbed by Tatenda”. Tatenda is the accused. This evidence is a dying declaration.

  2. A dying declaration is hearsay evidence, and its admissibility is regulated by s 254 of the Criminal Procedure and Evidence Act [Chapter 9:07] and the common law. The common law requirements for the admissibility of a dying declaration are these:

  1. the declarant must have died;

  2. the statement can only be used to prove the way the deceased met his or her death;

  3. the declarant must have had a settled hopeless expectation of death;

  4. and, further that he or she would have been a competent witness at the time of the declaration.

  1. In the present case, the deceased would have been a competent witness at the time he made the statement. He was under a settled, hopeless expectation of death in that he had just been stabbed with a knife and was bleeding profusely. Therefore, the requirements of admissibility of a dying declaration are satisfied in this case, and the statement that “I have been stabbed by Tatenda” is admissible in evidence.

  2. The dying declaration is corroborated by the confirmed, warned, and cautioned statement of the accused. In his statement, he states that: “I admit to the charge levelled against me; the now deceased insulted me as to why I had taken some alcohol, as well as striking me with a brick, and that is what prompted me to stab the now deceased with a knife.”

  3. In summary, the evidence is that the deceased went out of the kitchen hut to reprimand the accused for hurling obscenities. After a short while, he returned with a stab wound and said it was the accused who stabbed him. In addition, the accused, in his confirmed, warned, and cautioned statement, admitted that he stabbed the deceased. Further, in his defence outline, the accused states that while defending himself, he stabbed the deceased. In the circumstances, his version in court that the deceased stabbed himself by mistake is not only improbable but is false beyond a reasonable doubt.

  4. The inference that the accused stabbed the deceased is consistent with all proven facts, and it excludes every other reasonable inference. Therefore, we are satisfied that the totality of the facts and the evidence adduced in this trial shows that the injuries on the deceased were caused by the accused. The post-mortem report shows that the injuries inflicted by the accused caused the death of the deceased.

  5. In his defence, the accused pleads self-defence. In terms of our law, the defence of self-defence has been codified in s 253 of the Criminal Law Code. In terms of s 253(2) of the Criminal Law Code, in determining whether or not the requirements of self-defence have been satisfied, a court shall take due account of the circumstances in which the accused found himself, including any knowledge or capability he may have had and any stress or fear that may have been operating on his mind. The accused’s defence of self-defence must be examined to determine whether he has met the requirements of the law. See S v Ndlovu 2021 (1) ZLR 826 (H).

  6. In this country, the jurisprudence shows that the requirements of the defence of self-defence are these:

  1. an unlawful attack;

  2. upon the accused or a third party, where the accused intervenes to protect that third party;

  3. the attack must have commenced or be imminent;

  4. the action taken must be necessary to arrest the attack; and

  5. the means used to avert the attack must be reasonable.



  1. The first question is, was there an unlawful attack? The accused’s version in court is that a fight started between him and the deceased, and he felt overpowered. He tried to flee, but the deceased held him by the belt, and the deceased pulled a knife from the accused's pocket and opened it. The deceased tried to stab him on the chest; he evaded the stab and pushed the deceased, who fell. By the time he fell, he had accidentally stabbed himself on the chest. He stabbed himself by mistake. The deceased pulled the knife from the chest and threw it to the ground, and the accused picked. The accused’s version is fictional. It is false that the deceased stabbed himself by mistake. Ngonidzashe Ruocha, who was a few meters away, could have heard the noise from the fight between the accused and the deceased. She heard nothing. This version does not accord with the probabilities of the case, and beyond a reasonable doubt is false.

  2. In his defence outline, which is before the court marked Annexure A, the accused elaborates his defence as follows:

  1. “At approximately 2000 hours, the accused returned to his homestead, situated a few meters from that of the deceased. While setting a trap to catch mice, the accused was suddenly struck by a stone, causing him to fall and sustain bleeding injuries.

  2. Upon regaining his footing, the accused pursued the assailant.

  3. The accused caught up with the assailant, and a physical struggle ensued. During the course of the struggle, the accused realised that the assailant was the deceased. The deceased then took a knife from the accused’s pocket and attempted to stab him.

  4. The accused, acting instinctively and in fear for his life, wrestled the knife from the deceased. In the course of defending himself, the accused stabbed the deceased once.” (My emphasis)


  1. His version in his defence outline is that the deceased struck him with a stone and fled from the scene. The accused chased him and got up with him, and a physical altercation ensued. We find that the evidence that the deceased fled is false, because if he did so, he could have run towards his homestead, which was a few meters away. Ngonidzashe Ruocha, who was in the

kitchen hut, would have witnessed this; she did not. In addition, if indeed the deceased fled, the accused would have mentioned this important fact in his confirmed, warned, and cautioned statement. He did not.

  1. The evidence of a physical struggle between the accused and the deceased is false. This is so because in his statement, he said the deceased struck him with a brick, and that is what prompted him to stab him with a knife. There is no mention of a physical struggle. In any event, if ever there was a physical struggle, Ngonidzashe Ruocha could have heard the noise emanating from that struggle. She heard nothing. She only saw the deceased returning to the kitchen hut with a stab wound.

  2. The evidence that the deceased struck the accused with a stone does not seem to be true. Even if we give the accused the benefit of the doubt and accept, in his favour, that the deceased struck him with a stone, it just provides him with cold comfort. We can give him the benefit of doubt because the deceased must have been angry that the accused was shouting obscenities. In addition, the accused mentioned in his confirmed statement that he was struck, although he said it was a brick. Even if we accept this part of his evidence, it will not mean that the accused was under an unlawful attack. This is so because in his defence outline, he says, “upon regaining his footing, the accused pursued the assailant.” The defence outline is binding on an accused because it is prepared at his behest and usually read over to him. See Ephias Chigova v State 1992 (2) ZLR 206 at 213 C to F. When he, according to his version, pursued the deceased, he was not under attack but was seeking revenge.

  3. The prosecution has proved its case, and the totality of the evidence and the probabilities of the case show that at the moment the accused stabbed the deceased, he was not under an attack. According to his own version, he was seeking revenge. Revenge is not self-defence. The accused's defence fails on the first hurdle that must be satisfied in a case of self-defence, that is, whether he was under an unlawful attack. Once the prosecution proves that an accused was not under attack, or an unlawful one, the other requirements of self-defence fall by the wayside. Therefore, the defence of self-defence as codified in s 253 of the Criminal Law Code is not available to the accused in whatever form as a defence.

  4. The accused stabbed the deceased with a lethal weapon, a knife. He targeted the chest. He used excessive force, resulting in a stab wound of approximately 3 cm deep as depicted in the post-mortem report. The chest is the command centre of the human body. It protects and shields vital organs of the body such as the heart, lungs, liver, and major blood vessels. No human being can survive such a violent knife attack directed at the chest. No wonder the deceased died

soon after he was stabbed. This is a case of murder. In stabbing the deceased with a knife and targeting the chest, the accused foresaw death as a substantially certain result of that activity and proceeded regardless of whether death ensued. See S v Mugwanda 2002 (1) ZLR 547 (S); S v Tailor & Anor HB-126-22.

  1. Having carefully weighed the evidence adduced in this trial, we find that the State has proved its case beyond a reasonable doubt against the accused. The accused person is charged with murder as defined in s 47(1) of the Criminal Law Code. It is no longer necessary in our law to specify whether the accused is guilty of murder in terms of s 47(1) (a) or (b). See Mapfoche & Another v The State SC 84/21.

  2. In the result, the accused is found guilty of murder as defined in s 47 (1) of the Criminal Law (Codification & Reform) Act [Chapter 9:23].

Sentence

  1. Mr Maturure, this court has convicted you of murder as defined in s 47(1) of the Criminal Law Code. This court has found that in the reading of ss 47(2) and (3) of the Criminal Law Code, the murder you have been convicted of was not committed in aggravating circumstances. The court must now decide what sentence is appropriate for the offence for which you have been found guilty. To arrive at the appropriate sentence to be imposed, this Court will look at your personal circumstances, consider the nature of the offence, and the interests of society.

  2. The accused did not lead evidence in mitigation of sentence. He placed the following personal circumstances before the court through the medium of his legal practitioner. He is 22 years old now. He was 21 years old at the time of the commission of this offence. He is not married. Prior to his arrest, he was doing casual jobs, earning between USD$50 and $100 per month. His parents are deceased. He stays alone at his deceased grandmother’s homestead. He has no property of value. At the date of sentence, he has been in custody for just over a year. He is a first offender. There is no evidence that this murder was premeditated. It was just a spontaneous reaction to the reprimand directed at the accused to stop hurling obscenities. This was compounded by youthfulness and drunkenness on the part of the accused. We have also given him the benefit of the doubt that the deceased struck him with a stone. The court must weigh these mitigating features against the aggravating factors and the interests of justice.

  3. The accused used a knife, a very dangerous weapon. He stabbed the deceased on the chest with excessive force. The post mortem report speaks to the force used by the accused in stabbing the deceased. The life of a sixty-four-year-old man was ended. He left behind a devastated spouse and an orphaned 5-year-old child. The manner the deceased was killed was

brutal and shows disregard to human life. The deceased died a painful death. This is a bad case of murder. It is incumbent on this court to emphasize the sanctity of human life. Society frowns at the taking of another man’s life, particularly with a weapon like a knife to the chest. The accused’s moral blameworthiness is very high indeed. It is the duty of the court to ensure that those who by their hands violently cause the death of other human beings, are held accountable.

  1. The accused says he is remorseful. We do not agree. His post-murder behaviour should be considered when one assesses whether the accused is remorseful. Many accused persons might well regret their conduct but that does not, without more, translate to genuine remorse. Remorse is a gnawing pain of conscience for the plight of another. This genuine contrition can only come from an appreciation and acknowledgement of the extent of one's error. Whether the offender is sincerely remorseful and not simply feeling sorry for himself or herself at having been caught is a factual question. It is to the surrounding actions of the accused, rather than what he says in court that one should rather look at. For the remorse to be a valid consideration the pertinence must be sincere, and the accused must take the court fully into his or her confidence. Until and unless that happens, the genuineness of the contrition alleged to exist cannot be determined, after all before a court can find that an accused person is genuinely remorseful, it needs to have a proper appreciation of inter alia what motivated the accused to commit the deed, what has since provoked his or her change of heart and whether he or she does indeed have a true appreciation of the consequences of those actions. He must take responsibility for his actions and stop attributing false blame to the deceased. S v Matyityi [2010] 2 All SA 424 (SCA) at para 13; S v Rabie 1975 (4) SA 855 (A). The accused

was not genuine remorseful. He was just regretting and feeling pity for himself.

  1. In cases of serious crime such as this, the personal circumstances of the offenders by themselves will necessarily recede in the background. The sentence must speak to this phenomenon.


  1. After taking all the relevant factors into account, we are of the view that the following sentence will meet the justice of this case:


The accused is sentenced to 15 years' imprisonment.
