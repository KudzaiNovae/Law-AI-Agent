NDUNA J: This is an application for a declaratur concerning the forfeiture of a registered mining claim known as Dunraven 9, Registration Number PM 21365, situated in Shurugwi, Midlands Province. The Applicant in these proceedings is Vandai Ngorima, who approaches the court seeking relief against the Minister of Mines and Mining Development, the Secretary for Mines and Mining Development, and the Provincial Mining Director for Midlands Province.

FACTUAL BACKGROUND

This matter arises from a dispute concerning the forfeiture of a registered mining claim known as Dunraven 9, Registration Number PM 21365, situated in Shurugwi, Midlands Province. The Applicant in these proceedings is Vandai Ngorima, who approaches the court seeking relief against the Minister of Mines and Mining Development, the Secretary for Mines and Mining Development, and the Provincial Mining Director for Midlands Province, all cited in their official capacities.

The mining claim in question has a long registration history, having originally been registered on 25 October 1994, as evidenced by the Certificate of Registration attached to the court papers. Over the years, the claim changed hands through private agreements of sale. On 8 May 2014, Falcon Gold Zimbabwe Limited, which was at the time reflected in official records as the registered holder of the claim, sold Dunraven 9 to Busywork Dube for a purchase price of USD 3,450, pursuant to a written Agreement of Sale. Subsequently, on 13 September 2017, Busywork Dube entered into a Memorandum of Agreement of Sale with the Applicant, Vandai Ngorima, in terms of which the Applicant purchased the same mining claim for USD 4,000. Copies of both agreements of sale were placed before the court and annexed to the Applicant’s founding affidavit.

The Applicant’s case is premised on the assertion that by virtue of these agreements of sale, he became the lawful owner and beneficial holder of Dunraven 9, with a direct and substantial interest in the claim. He avers that he took occupation and assumed responsibility for the claim, including the obligation to comply with statutory requirements under the Mines and Minerals Act [Chapter 21:05].

On 18 January 2019, the Respondents, acting through the Ministry of Mines and Mining Development, declared Dunraven 9 forfeited. The forfeiture was purportedly effected in terms of section 260 of the Mines and Minerals Act, on the basis that the holder of the claim had failed to obtain an inspection or protection certificate. A Notice of Forfeiture, listing Dunraven 9 among other claims, was issued and displayed at the offices of the Ministry. The Applicant contends that this forfeiture came as a complete surprise to him, as he had not been served with any prior written notice of the intention to forfeit the claim, nor had he been afforded an opportunity to regularise the alleged non-compliance by paying any outstanding inspection fees.

The gravamen of the Applicant’s complaint is that the forfeiture was unlawful, unreasonable, and procedurally unfair. He asserts that section 260 of the Mines and Minerals Act requires that a mining title holder be given notice of the intended forfeiture and an opportunity to be heard before such a drastic administrative action is taken. In addition, the Applicant relies on the Administrative Justice Act [Chapter 10:28] and the Constitution of Zimbabwe, arguing that the Respondents were under a duty to act lawfully, reasonably, and in a fair manner, which includes respecting the audi alteram partem rule.

The Applicant further contends that merely placing a notice on a Ministry notice board does not constitute adequate or effective notice, particularly in circumstances where the law allows an affected party to apply for the revocation of forfeiture within a prescribed timeframe. He argues that without personal or direct notice, he was deprived of the opportunity to exercise this statutory right.

As a result of the forfeiture, the Respondents allege that Dunraven 9 was subsequently re-pegged by Magau Mining Syndicate, which they submit lawfully acquired rights to the claim following its forfeiture.

In opposing the application, the 3rd Respondent, the Provincial Mining Director for Midlands Province, states that according to official records held by the Ministry, Falcon Gold Zimbabwe Limited remained the registered holder of Dunraven 9 at the time of forfeiture. The Respondents aver that there is no record of any approved or registered transfer of the mining claim from Falcon Gold Zimbabwe Limited to Busywork Dube, nor from Busywork Dube to the Applicant. On this basis, the Respondents contend that the Applicant has no locus standi to challenge the forfeiture, as he was never the registered title holder recognised under the Act.

The Respondents further argue that the forfeiture was validly effected in terms of sections 260, 271, and 272 of the Mines and Minerals Act, due to the failure to pay requisite fees and to obtain an inspection or protection certificate. They maintain that no address for service had been registered in respect of the claim, as required by section 60 of the Act, either by Falcon Gold Zimbabwe Limited or by the Applicant. Consequently, they argue that the posting of the forfeiture notice on the Ministry’s notice board constituted deemed personal service in law. In support of this position, the Respondents rely on the decision in Onesimo Mazai Moyo & Others v Anesu Gold (Pvt) Ltd SC 100/24, which they cite as authority for the proposition that notice by posting is sufficient where no address for service has been registered.

The Respondents seek to distinguish the matter from Fidelity Printers and Refineries (Pvt) Ltd v Minister of Mines & Others SC 107/22, contending that in that case there was a registered address for service and identifiable title holder, whereas in the present matter the absence of a registered address rendered notice board posting procedurally compliant.

In reply, the Applicant persists in his contention that he has a direct and substantial interest in the mining claim arising from the agreements of sale and that this interest entitles him to approach the court for relief. He argues that the Respondents have failed to adequately address the issue of procedural fairness, and that their silence or avoidance on this point amounts to a tacit admission that the forfeiture process was flawed. The Applicant places strong reliance on the Fidelity Printers decision, which he submits authoritatively established that forfeiture of mining rights without prior personal notice and an opportunity to be heard is unlawful, regardless of administrative convenience.

Finally, the Applicant contends that because the forfeiture was unlawful, any subsequent re-pegging or allocation of Dunraven 9 to Magau Mining Syndicate is a nullity and confers no legal rights. On this basis, he seeks a declaratory order setting aside the forfeiture, an order permitting him to pay the outstanding inspection fees within 90 days, and an award of costs on the attorney and client scale.

ISSUES FOR DETERMINATION

  1. Whether the Applicant is an interested person entitled to seek a declaratory order.

  2. Whether the forfeiture was procedurally proper under Section 260 of the Mines and Minerals Act and The Administration Justice Act

APPLICATION OF THE LAW TO THE FACTS

The Applicant approaches the court seeking a declaratory order in terms of section 14 of the High Court Act which states

“The High court may, in its discretion at the instance of any interested person enquire into and determine any existing, future or contigent right on obligation, notwithstanding that such person cannot claim any relief consequential upon such determination”.

The nature and purpose of a declaratory order have been authoritatively explained by Herbstein & Van Winsen: explained the legal remedy of a declaratory order in the following terms:―

“A declaratory order is an order by which a dispute over the existence of some legal right or obligation is resolved․ The right or obligation can be existing, prospective or contingent and no specific performance need be claimed”

Section 14 of the High Court Act vests the court with a discretion to enquire into and determine any existing, future, or contingent right or obligation at the instance of an interested person. The jurisprudence is settled that before such discretion can be exercised, a condition precedent must be satisfied, namely that the applicant must demonstrate a direct and substantial interest in the subject matter of the dispute.This principle was clearly articulated in Munn Publishing (Pvt) Ltd v ZBC 1994 (1) ZLR 337 (S), where the Supreme Court held

“that an interested person is one whose rights are not remote or academic, but are capable of being prejudicially affected by the court’s determination”.

Applying this principle to the present matter, the Applicant’s interest arises from agreements of sale concluded with Busywork Dube, who himself allegedly purchased the mining claim from Falcon Gold Zimbabwe Limited. The official records of the Ministry of Mines do not reflect any registered transfer from Falcon Gold Zimbabwe Limited to Busywork Dube, nor from Busywork Dube to the Applicant. However, the agreements of sale placed before the court have not been challenged for validity by any of the parties. In the absence of such challenge, they remain binding inter partes and confer upon the Applicant a real and substantial proprietary interest in the mining claim, sufficient to clothe him with standing as the owner for purposes of these proceedings.

Whether the Forfeiture Was Procedurally Proper Under Section 260 of the Mines and Minerals Act and The Administration Justice Act

The forfeiture in issue was effected in terms of section 260 of the Mines and Minerals Act, which provides that failure to obtain an inspection certificate within the prescribed period renders the mining block liable to forfeiture, unless a protection certificate has been obtained where it states

“260․ Forfeiture for failure to obtain inspecting certificate for block

“Failure to obtain an inspection certificate within the period prescribed therefore shall unless a protection certificate has been obtained under section two hundred and seventeen in respect of such block, render liable to forfeiture the block in respect of which such failure has taken place”․

The Supreme Court in Fidelity Printers and Refiners (Pvt) Ltd v Minister of Mines and Mining Development N.O & Others SC 107/22 clarified that the phrase “liable to forfeiture” does not connote automatic forfeiture by operation of law. Rather, forfeiture remains a discretionary administrative act, which must be exercised in accordance with the Act where it was held

“The fate of this appeal hinges on the interpretation of s 260 of the Act, in particular the meaning of the words “render liable to forfeiture” it also hinges on the interpretation of s 272 of the Act as read with s 3 of the Administrative Justice Act․ Given their ordinary grammatical meaning these words do not connote automatic forfeiture by operation of law as contended by the respondents․ The words used by the legislature in this section simply mean that the mining block in respect of which the statutory fee has not been paid is susceptible for forfeiture․ It may be forfeited at the discretion of the Mining Commissioner․

In our view s 260 does not provide automatic forfeiture․ It merely renders the mining block liable or open to forfeiture in terms of this Act, the Mining Commissioner may declare such location to be forfeited․”

The Applicant places reliance on Fidelity to challenge the forfeiture. However, a careful examination of that decision reveals that the present matter is materially distinguishable. In Fidelity, the registered holder of the mining location had furnished a valid address for service as required by section 60 of the Act, yet the administrative authority failed to give personal notice of the intended forfeiture or an opportunity to be heard. The Supreme Court held that such failure rendered the forfeiture procedurally unfair and unlawful.

By contrast, in the present matter, it is common cause that no address for service was ever furnished to the Mining Commissioner by the registered title holder, Falcon Gold Zimbabwe Limited, nor by the Applicant. The providing of an address is required by law and is guided by Section 60 of the Mines and Minerals Act where it reads

“60 Address to be given to mining commissioner

1) Every holder of a mining location on registration of such location in his name at the office of the mining commissioner and every lessee and assignee of such holder shall furnish such mining commissioner with an address within Zimbabwe at which all notices, orders /or other process shall be served by the mining commissioner or the officer duly appointed for the purposes of this Act, and any such holder, lessee or assignee may at any time change such address by registering at the office of such mining commissioner or any office of such mining commissioner at any other address within Zimbabwe․

2) Service of any such notice or order or other process with such registered address shall be deemed to have the same effect as personal service․

3) In default of any address being registered as required by this section, the posting in the office of the mining commissioner of any such notice order or other process shall be deemed to have the same effect as personal service․

4) Nothing in this section contained shall be construed so as to preclude the High Court from giving such directions with regard to service as to it seem proper and expedient․”

This section imposes a statutory obligation on every holder of a mining location, lessee, or assignee to furnish the Mining Commissioner with an address within Zimbabwe for service of notices. The section further provides that where no address has been registered, posting notices at the office of the Mining Commissioner shall be deemed to have the same effect as personal service.

In the present case, the Respondents acted strictly in accordance with section 60(3) by posting the forfeiture notices on the Ministry’s notice board. Such service is, by statute, deemed to be personal service. The Applicant cannot therefore complain of lack of notice when the failure to receive direct notice was occasioned by non-compliance with a clear statutory obligation.

This position finds direct support in Moyo N.O & Others v Anesu Gold (Pvt) Ltd & Another SC 100/24, a case whose facts closely mirror those of the present matter. In Moyo, the Supreme Court on para 72 held that

“The first respondent failed to obtain an inspection certificate within the period prescribed by law․ Section 260 of the Act is instructive․ Such failure to obtain relevant certification rendered the claim liable to forfeiture․ The first respondent’s claims were liable to forfeiture due to non-certification․ Further, the first respondent did not furnish the Ministry with an address thereby prompting the Ministry to lawfully post the forfeiture notices on the notice boards․ Such service, as earlier discussed amounts to proper personal service in terms of s 60 (3) of the Act․ It therefore follows that the first respondent was lawfully given an opportunity to make representations and take requisite legal steps to prevent the claims from being open to relocation”․

The evidence before the court establishes that at the time Dunraven 9 was declared liable to forfeiture, Falcon Gold Zimbabwe Limited remained the registered holder of the mining claim and that no transfer into the name of Busywork Dube or the Applicant had ever been registered. Critically, neither Falcon Gold nor the Applicant furnished the Mining Commissioner with an address for service as required by section 60(1) of the Mines and Minerals Act. Despite alleging ownership from 2017, the Applicant never regularised his position with the Ministry, did not register as an assignee, and did not place himself on record as a person entitled to receive statutory notices. The administrative authority was therefore confined to dealing with the claim strictly on the basis of the official mining register.

In the absence of a registered address, section 60(3) of the Minerals Act became operative, permitting the Mining Commissioner to post notices at the Ministry offices, with such posting being deemed to have the same effect as personal service. The Respondents followed this procedure by posting the forfeiture notice listing Dunraven 9 on the Ministry notice board. This method of service was not a discretionary choice but a statutory consequence of non-compliance with section 60.

These facts align squarely with the Supreme Court’s reasoning in Moyo N.O & Others v Anesu Gold (Pvt) Ltd SC 24/100, where it was held that failure to register an address lawfully entitles the Ministry to effect service by notice-board posting, which constitutes proper personal service in law. The present matter is therefore distinguishable from Fidelity Printers, where the registered holder had provided an address and the administrative authority failed to use it. Here, the Respondents complied fully with section 60, afforded a lawful opportunity for compliance, and the forfeiture followed only after continued inaction. The Applicant’s complaint of lack of notice is thus unsustainable.

This however is not the end of the story the law requires the forfeiture to be administratively just. Section 260 of the Minerals Act should be read along with Section 3 of the Administrative Justice Act [Chapter 10:28] which obliges administrative authorities to act lawfully, reasonably, and in a fair manner where it provides as follows

3 Duty of administrative authority

An administrative authority which has the responsibility or power to take any administrative action which may affect the rights, interests and or legitimate expectations of any person shall :—

a) act lawfully, reasonably and in a fair manner; and

b) act within the relevant period specified by law or if there is no such specified period within a reasonable period after being requested to take the action by the person concerned; and

c) where it has taken the action, supply written reasons therefor within the relevant period specified by law or, if there is no such specified period within a reasonable period after being requested to supply reasons by the person concerned․

In order for an administrative action to be taken in a fair manner as required by paragraph (a) of subs (1) an administrative authority shall give a person referred to in subs (1) :—

(a) adequate notice of the nature and purpose of the proposed action; and

(b) a reasonable opportunity to make adequate representation; and

(c) adequate notice of any right of renew or appeal where applicable․

An administrative authority may depart from any of the requirements referred to in subsection (1) or (2) if :-

the enactment under which the decision is made expressly provides for any matters referred to in those subsections so as to vary or exclude any of their requirements; or

the departure is, under the circumstances reasonable and justifiable, in which case the administrative authority shall take into account all relevant matters, including :—

(i) the objects of the applicable enactment or rule of common law;

(ii) the likely effect of its action;

(iii) the urgency of the matter or the urgency of acting thereon;

(iv) the need to promote efficient administration and good governance;

(v) the need to promote public interest․”

The Act itself permits departures from these requirements where such departure is reasonable and justifiable, taking into account factors such as the object of the enactment, the need for efficient administration, and the promotion of public interest.

In Moyo supra, the Supreme Court confirmed that where forfeiture notices are lawfully posted in terms of section 60(3), and the affected party is afforded a defined period within which to regularise non-compliance or make representations, the requirements of procedural fairness are satisfied.

In the present matter, the notices posted by the Ministry clearly indicated that the mining claim was liable to forfeiture due to failure to obtain an inspection certificate. The registered holder was afforded a twenty-one-day period within which to take corrective action. No steps were taken within that period. The forfeiture therefore proceeded as a direct consequence of continued non-compliance.

Put differently, the Respondents did not act arbitrarily or capriciously. They followed the statutory framework, afforded a reasonable opportunity for compliance, and only proceeded to forfeit the claim after that opportunity had been ignored. In these circumstances, the administrative action was not only lawful, but also reasonable and fair within the meaning of the Administrative Justice Act.

Disposition

The Applicant has failed to establish procedural irregularity in the forfeiture of mining claim Dunraven 9 (PM 21365). The forfeiture was lawfully and reasonably effected in terms of sections 260 and 60(3) of the Mines and Minerals Act, read with section 3 of the Administrative Justice Act.

The application is dismissed with costs at ordinary scale.
