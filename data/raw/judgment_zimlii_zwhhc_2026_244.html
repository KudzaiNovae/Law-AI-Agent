Introduction

MUCHAWA J: The accused stands charged with two counts being murder as defined in section 47(1) of the Criminal Law (Codification and Reform) Act [Chapter 9:23] and attempted murder as defined in section 189 as read with section 47(1) of the same Act.

The allegations in respect of count one are that on 27 December 2024 and at New Stands Whitecliff South, Zvimba, the accused unlawfully and with intent or realizing the attendant risk that his conduct may cause death, continued to so engage by assaulting Tinashe Shayi all over the body with fists, booted feet, sticks, a rope, and iron rod thereby causing injuries from which Tinashe Shayi died.

For count two, it is alleged that on 27 December 2024 and at New Stands, Whitecliff South, Zvimba, the accused unlawfully and with an intent to kill or realizing the attendant risk continued in assaulting Everisto Sachikonye with fists, booted feet, sticks, a rope and an iron rod all over the body thereby causing him some injuries.

The accused pleaded not guilty to both counts. Though he agrees to being present when Dryton Mukandi fetched Everisto Sachikonye and assaulted him, he claims to have stopped Mukandi, advised him to report to the police and retired to sleep. He also accompanied Mukandi to fetch the deceased and was there when the deceased was assaulted by Mukandi. Similarly, he intervened and stopped the assault and retired to bed. He therefore never participated in any assault and denies any complicity in the perpetration of the offences and prayed for his acquittal.

Common Cause Facts.

The accused, in his defence outline states that he gave his statement to the police freely and voluntarily without any illicit pressure being brought to bear on him. He incorporated the contents of his confirmed warned and cautioned statement into his defence outline. The defence outline was also incorporated into evidence.

Section 256 (2) of the Criminal Procedure and Evidence Act [Chapter 9:07] provides that a confession or statement confirmed in terms of Section one hundred and thirteen shall be received in evidence before any court upon its mere production by the prosecutor without further proof.

In this case the following facts emerge from exhibit 2 as common cause:

1. There is a strong association between the accused and Mukandi.

a. They were close neighbours.

b. They confided in each other. In this case Mukandi confided in the accused about the loss of his blanket and gas tank.

c. The accused willingly accompanied Mukandi in hunting for Sachikonye and located him at pama Grounds shops.

d. The association was strengthened by the fact that the accused claimed to have also lost a trowel to Sachikonye’s thieving.

e. The accused was present at Mukandi’s cabin when Sachikonye was assaulted. In evidence, he clarified this was for three and half hours.

f. The accused accompanied Mukandi to deceased’s house and collected him and brought him to Mukandi’s house where the assaults continued in his presence.

2. It is common cause that Sachikonye was assaulted at Mukandi’s place before the deceased was fetched.

3. Exhibit 2 confirms that dangerous weapons were used in the assault, an iron rod / bar.

4. It is common cause that Sachikonye was staying with the accused and doing some construction work for him at the material time.

Other common cause factors emerging from the overall evidence are as follows:

1. There was a woman at or near Mukandi’s cabin who told the deceased’s relatives that he had departed from the cabin at 5:00am on 27 December 2024. This was in the presence of the police, and it prompted the attending police officers to depart without any further investigations.

2. It is common cause that the deceased’s body was discovered in Mukandi’s cabin, the place he had been delivered to after his collection by the accused, Mukandi, Sachikonye and Samutoko.

3. From the accused’s warned and cautioned statement, he witnessed Mukandi assaulting the deceased using an iron rod. He was therefore present.

Disputed Issues

The accused disputes the following;

1. That he assaulted the deceased and Sachikonye as alleged or at all.

2. That there is a valid postmortem report.

Whether there is a valid postmortem report.

It is defence counsel’s submission that the postmortem report which is in the form of an affidavit and does not show the identity of the commissioner of oaths exfacio the document is not valid. It is argued that there is therefore no postmortem report. The court was referred to the case of Prosecutor General V Edmore Makarichi &ORS HH 502/23. This case was an opposed court application wherein the validity of the supporting affidavits was challenged. This was on the basis that the supposed Commissioner of Oaths was not disclosed as the name of the purported Commissioner of Oaths is not disclosed. Above the designation “Commissioner of Oaths” appears not the name of the purported commissioner of oaths but a signature and handwritten endorsement suggesting that the signatory is a detective inspector. The stamp used is for the ZRP Officer-In-Charge, Team One Criminal Investigation Department. Further, the office or capacity in which the unknown signatory was acting as the purported Commissioner of Oaths is not disclosed.

In the above case, the court found that what was placed before it fell far short of the legal requirements. It was not an affidavit. This conclusion was reached after considering cases such as Tawanda V Ndebele 2006(1) ZLR 426 (H) and Firstel Cellular (Pvt) Ltd V Netone Cellular (Pvt) Ltd 2015 (1) ZLR 94 (S).

The case before me is distinct. It relates to the application of the Criminal Procedure and Evidence Act (Chapter 9:07) instead of civil procedure. Section 278 (1) (a) of the Criminal Procedure and Evidence Act provides as follows

“278 Admissibility of Affidavits in certain circumstances”.

(2) In any criminal proceedings in which it is relevant to prove –

(a) any fact ascertained by a medical practitioner in any examination carried out by him which is proper to the duties of a medical practitioner,

(b) that any treatment, including the performance of an operation, was administered by a medical practitioner,

(c) any opinion of a medical practitioner referred to in paragraph (a) or (b) relating to any fact of treatment referred to:

a document purporting to be an affidavit relating to any such examinations or treatment and purporting to have been made by a person who in that affidavit states that he is or was a medical practitioner and in the performance of his duties in that capacity he carried out such examination and ascertained such fact in such examination or administered such treatment, and in either case, arrived at such opinion, if any, stated therein shall, on its mere production in those proceedings by any person, but subject to subsections (11) and 12, be prima facie proof of the facts and of any opinion so stated.” ( My emphasis)

The postmortem report falls within the ambit of section 278 (2) of the Criminal Procedure and Evidence Act as was prepared by Doctor Ariana Pedro Courguet, a registered medical practitioner.

The use of the term “a document purporting to be an affidavit …. purporting to have been made by a person who in the affidavit states that he is or was a medical practitioner ….” is very intentional selection of language by the legislature. It does not say “an affidavit”. The very literal interpretation is that the document is a written and signed statement that appears or claims to be a sworn, notarized or legally authorized declaration of facts. In legal terms “purporting to be” means the document looks like or claims to be an affidavit but its validity or accuracy has not yet been fully verified or tested in court.

Section 278 (2) provides that such document shall, on its mere production in the proceedings, be prima facie proof of the facts. This is so, unless the provisions of section 278 (12) are set in motion to verify or test its validity.

In terms of S278 (12).

“The court in which any affidavit referred to in this section is produced in evidence, may, of its own motion or at the request of the prosecutor or of the accused, because the person who made the affidavit or any other person whose evidence the court considers to be necessary to give oral evidence in the proceedings in question in relation to any statement contained in the affidavit or may cause written interrogatories to be submitted to such person for reply, and such interrogatories or reply thereto purporting to be a reply from such person, shall, on their mere production in those proceedings by any person, be admissible in evidence”.

In casu there was no resort to section 278 (12) by the court and either party.

It is therefore our finding that exhibit 1, the postmortem report, by its mere production is/shall be prima facie proof of the facts stated.

Dr Arian Pedro Courguet concluded that the death of the deceased was caused by pneumothorax secondary to severe thoracic trauma. We accept this fact.

Whether the accused participated in the assault of Sachikonye and the deceased.

This is the deciding factor in this matter.

Though the defence sought to paint Sachikonye, the only witness who was present during the assault, as an implausible witness, we did not find this to be true. This is particularly as aspects of his testimony were corroborated by the accused’s own testimony and his confirmed warned and cautioned statement. He has a psychologically threatened witness who went into hiding after the assault. He seems to have tried to protect the accused who was sheltering him and employing him as he was of no fixed abode by denying that accused accused him of stealing his trowel and failing to submit the medical affidavit to the police which would have demonstrated the nature of injuries he suffered. Though he tried to minimize the role played by the accused, he made clear that the accused participated actively in the assaults. We therefore accepted his evidence.

The first issue is that the accused was present when Sachikonye was fetched from the shops. He was there and took part in assaulting him from around 4:00p.m, he participated when Sachikonye was tied up on the hands and legs and the accused and Mukandi took turns to assault him using sticks and an iron rod. This went on till 12:00 midnight though he could not tell who used which weapon when.

Though the accused wanted to give the impression that he stood by whilst Mukandi only did the assaulting and then went to sleep, the timeline of this having started at 2: 30p.m and leaving around 6:00p.m does not explain what he was doing for 3 and ½ hours. He does not say what he did to stop Mukandi. We believe Sachikonye’s version that he was actively engaged in the assault.

The accused lied that Sachikonye had not stolen anything from him contrary to his confirmed statement that he had stolen a trowel. He therefore had motive which matched that of Mukandi who claimed to have had his goods stolen too. This explains their teaming up.

After going to bed the accused lamely explains that he joined Mukandi to fetch the deceased as he wanted to protect Sachikonye from Mukandi. How could this be as he had left Sachikonye in the hands of Mukandi who was assaulting him?

Sachikonye related how he was assaulted on the way to fetch the deceased and on the way back. This explains why they took 2 hours to reach a place which was 1km and 4 hours on the way back. He said he could not walk properly due to injuries emanating from the assault.

Samutoko witnessed the accused assaulting the deceased, taking turns to slap the deceased. Samutoko was however not present all the time so as to fully account for any assaults meted out by the accused on Sachikonye. Sachikonye also said he was not assaulted whilst at Samutoko’s place and at the deceased.

Sachikonye said upon arrival at Mukandi’s place they were both taken inside the cabin and the accused and Mukandi assaulted them. He was taken outside so they could work on the deceased. He witnessed the accused assaulting the deceased whilst in the cabin and when he was taken outside and the door was open as they took turns to assault them. The iron rod, sticks and electric cables were used.

The accused was lying in his evidence in chief when he said upon returning from fetching the deceased he immediately went to his home and did not reach Mukandi’s place. This is contrary to his confirmed statement where he said,

“We then went to the now deceased’s and took him back to that man’s house. When we got to his house this man started to assault the now deceased and Sachikonye using an iron bar”.

He was present and participated in the assault.

The evidence from Admire Jeche and Thokozile Sithole confirms that when they arrived at Mukandi’s cabin and he finally opened up he called the accused and said they had assaulted the deceased together.

The accused is said to have said they had been disciplining him. Even in his confirmed statement, the accused says he told the deceased’s relatives that they are the ones who collected him from his house.

We also rely on the provisions of section 196 A (1) of the Criminal Law Code. It holds two or more people accused of committing a crime in association with each other where evidence is led to show the requisite mens rea whether or not their conduct contributed directly in any way to the commission of the crime, as liable. They may be found to have the intention to commit the crime or knowledge that it would be committed or the realisation of the real risk or possibility that a crime of this kind would be committed. In which case they may be convicted as a co-perpetrator.

Section 196A (2) lists factors that are indicative to prove that two or more persons accused of committing a crime in association with each other had the requisite mens rea.

The first is that they were present at or in the immediate vicinity of the crime scene. In this case the accused places himself at the scene in both instances when Sachikonye was assaulted at the cabin and when the assault on the deceased occurred at the cabin. He was also available in fetching both victims. This directly implicates him in the commission of the crime.

Secondly, the co-perpetrators were associated together in any conduct which resulted in the crimes for which accused is charged. By his own evidence, the accused went with Mukandi hunting for Sachikonye. He then also went hunting for the deceased in the dead of the night dragging the injured Sachikonye and force marching Samutoko to lead them.

Mukandi and the accused jointly told a lie to the deceased’s relatives that the deceased had left well knowing he was in the cabin. The lie was bolstered by Netsai, Mukandi’s girlfriend and accused’s sister. This was to buy them time to disappear from the scene.

Count 1

Given the totality of the evidence, it is our finding that the accused had the requisite mens rea to commit murder as he realised the real risk or possibility that his conduct may cause death, but he continued in that conduct despite the risk or possibility.

We find him guilty of murder as defined in Section 47 (1) of the Criminal Law Code.

Count 2

Sachikonye was brutally assaulted to the point of being in a bad shape some 2 days later with bruises all over the body, swollen legs and unable to walk. He even says he still suffers from back ache.

Only a medical practitioner’s evidence would have enabled the court to assess the degree of the assault perpetrated on Sachikonye to make appropriate conclusions whether the accused did or omitted to do anything that had reached at least the commencement of the execution of a murder crime.

Sachikonye said although the deceased was assaulted all over the body indiscriminately, he was only assaulted from the neck downwards.

The evidence having only proved that the accused and his co-perpetrator committed an assault upon Sachikonye intending to cause him bodily harm or realising the attendant risk, we find the accused not guilty of attempted murder but guilty of assault as defined in Section 89 (1) of the Criminal Law Code.

Consequently, we order as follows:

Count 1

1. The accused is found guilty of murder as defined in Section 47 (1) of the Criminal Law Code.

Count 2

2. The accused is found not guilty of attempted murder but is convicted of assault as defined in Section 89 (1) of the Criminal Law Code.

Sentencing

The Law

The court is obliged to first decide on whether the offence of murder was committed in aggravating circumstances or not. The court will then be guided as to whether section 47 (4) (a) or (b) applies.

Where it is found that a murder was committed in aggravating circumstances, Section 47 (4) (a) provides for a penalty of imprisonment for life or any definite period of not less than twenty years. Where no aggravating factors are found, the penalty is imprisonment for life or any definite period of less than fifteen years or (if there are special circumstances) of not less than five years.

The sentencing guidelines SI 146/2023 set the presumptive penalty for aggravated murder at 20 years and the presumptive penalty at 15 years imprisonment for where mitigating factors exist.

In respect of assault, the penalty ranges from a fine up to or exceeding level fourteen, or imprisonment for a period not exceeding 10 years or both. One has to have regard to the factors set in S89 (3) of the Criminal Law Code such as the age and physical condition of the person assaulted, degree of force employed, extent of physical injury on the assaulted person, whether the intention was to inflict bodily harm, whether assailant was in a position of authority of the person assaulted.

The presumptive penalty for an assault committed in aggravating factors per the Sentencing Guidelines is 3 years imprisonment and where there are mitigating factors, it is a level 4 fine.

Mitigating Factors we found to exist.

We took note that the accused is a first offender, Section 9 (a) of the Sentencing Guidelines urges the court to take this into account as do several other case law authorities.

The personal circumstances of the accused are that he is a 35 year old man, widowed and father to two minor daughters aged 8 and 6 years. He is the sole breadwinner for his children and elderly mother aged 65 years.

Aggravating Factors Found to Exist.

The assaults on the two victims were brutal, prolonged and unjustified. The assault on Sachikonye started in the afternoon of 26 December 2024 into the early morning of 27 December 2024. That of the deceased started close to midnight on 26 December 2024 to early morning 27 December 2024.

The accused and his co-perpetrator used lethal weapons which included an iron rod, sticks, electric cable and open hands. Due to the brutal attacks, it took them up to 4 hours to walk a distance of about a kilometre as they dragged and assaulted the victims.

It was our finding that the accused was an equal participant in the assaults.

The assaults on the victims have been argued to constitute torture and are a violation of Article 1 of the United Nations Convention against Torture and other Cruel or Inhuman or Degrading Treatment or Punishment. This is particularly so as the assaults were intended to elicit confessions on the suspected stolen goods. See State v Reza & Another 2004 (1) ZLR 138(H) pp 142 H – 1143G and Petros Makaza & Another v State and Kumbozo Gumbo & Another v State CCZ 16/17 p3.

The two victims were tied up with a rope/electric cable on the hands and legs, they were not aggressive.

The accused and his co-perpetrator shut out any assistance which could have been rendered to the deceased who was in their custody. They warded the relatives away on the lie that the deceased had left to fetch the goods and even got a woman accomplice to tell this lie in the face of the police. Thereafter they put double locks to the cabin and made their escape good.

Life is sacrosanct. It is God given and constitutionally guaranteed. No one has the right to take the law into his hands and deal with another human as he pleases.

The accused was in the position of employer of Sachikonye and held a position of authority over him and even housed him. Instead of protecting Sachikonye he elected to turn villain.

The accused and his relatives have not apologised for the offences. They did not render any assistance at the funeral. The accused’s demeanor in court did not show any remorse.

The victim impact statement of Thokozile Sithole (deceased’s mother) shows the high level of trauma she has suffered from the discovery of blood at the cabin door to when the body was discovered. No mother should ever go through that. Her trauma was evident as she gave her evidence. Equally the brother Admire Jeche who watched helplessly as his brother was dragged to his death is traumatized. The family has been robbed of a breadwinner. The deceased left two minor children whom Sithole now has to fend for her.

Sachikonye has not received any pity or acknowledgement from the accused. He is still suffering pain on his back as a result of the assault and is now unable to fend for himself and has been relegated to a beggar.

It was not enough that they assaulted Sachikonye, they went in the dead of the night to fetch the deceased and bludgeoned him to death.

The Sentence.

The State proposed a sentence of 25 years imprisonment for count 1- and 8-years imprisonment of which 3 years is suspended for court 2 with the sentences running concurrently.

The defence prayed for a sentence of 15 years imprisonment, or any sentence not exceeding 20 years with a portion suspended on condition of good future behaviour. It is proposed that the sentences run concurrently.

The aggravating factors far outweigh the mitigating ones. The murder was committed in aggravating factors.

In the circumstances the following sentences are fitting.

Count 1 – 22 years’ imprisonment.

Count 2 – 5 years’ imprisonment with 3 years suspended for 5 years on condition that the accused does not, within that time commit an offence in which violence on the person of another is an element and for which he is sentenced without the option of a fine.

The sentences in count 1 and count 2 are to run concurrently. Effective 22 years’ imprisonment.
