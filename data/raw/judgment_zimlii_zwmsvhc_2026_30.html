EUVENCIO MUSUNDIRE


v


THE STATE


IN THE HIGH COURT OF ZIMBABWE DUEB-BANDA J

MASVINGO 29 May 2026


Application for condonation for the late noting of an appeal and extension of time within which to note an appeal and to prosecute an appeal in person

The applicant in person

E. Mbavarira, for the respondent


DUBE-BANDA J:


  1. This is a chamber application for condonation for leave to note an appeal outside the time allowed by the rules of court and to prosecute the appeal in person. After hearing submissions, I dismissed the application. The applicant has requested written reasons for the dismissal of his application. These are they.


  1. The background of this matter is that the applicant, together with 7 other persons, appeared before the Masvingo Regional Magistrates Court charged with the crime of robbery as defined in s 126 of the Criminal Law (Codification and Reform) Act [Chapter 9:23] “the Criminal Law Code”. It was alleged that the applicant and his co-accused persons, on 1 March 2022, unlawfully and intentionally used a firearm and pepper spray to subdue an employee of the Empress Mine to relinquish a bucket containing one thousand grammes of gold ore concentrate.


  1. The applicant and his co-accused pleaded not guilty, and after a contested trial, the applicant and 6 of them were convicted of robbery and sentenced to twelve years' imprisonment, with 2 suspended for 5 years on the usual conditions of good behaviour. Although the applicant says he was aggrieved by his conviction and sentence, he did not note an appeal within the time allowed by the rules of court. Hence this application for condonation and extension of time within which to note an appeal and leave to prosecute the appeal in person.




The submissions by the parties


  1. In his founding affidavit in support of this application, the applicant contended that the delay in approaching this court was not willful; it was occasioned by circumstances that were beyond his control. He avers that he did not know that he had a right of appeal and that there was a timeline to file an appeal. He contends that he got to know of the right to appeal after his transfer from Masvingo Prison to Chikurubi Prison. He further encountered a challenge in obtaining a copy of the transcribed record because it had to be paid for by someone outside prison.


  1. In his written application, he argued that he has very good prospects of success on appeal against conviction because the case was poorly investigated. He also seems to challenge the evidence of identification and indications and the trial court’s evaluation of the evidence. He emphasized that he did not commit the offence. Regarding sentence, he argued that it is so severe as to induce a deep sense of shock. He argued that the trial court did not pay regard to the fact that he was a first offender, and that nothing was stolen because of the robbery, and that the court did not consider restitution. In support of his application to prosecute the appeal in person, the applicant contended that he has no resources to enable him to brief a legal practitioner.


  1. The State opposed the application for condonation and argued that it has no merit and ought to be dismissed. The respondent argued that the extent of the delay is inordinate. Regarding conviction, it was submitted that in his defense outline he admitted that he was at the scene of the crime on a mission to rob the mine workers of gold. It was further argued that he admitted that he took a gun from the security guard and that he was positively identified, and the role he played in the commission of the crime was outlined. It was submitted further that because the applicant was identified by a witness who knew him, there was no need for an identification parade. It was contended that the trial court cannot be faulted for admitting into evidence that the applicant and his accomplices led to the recovery of the firearm used in the commission of the robbery. In respect of the sentence, it was submitted that robbery is a serious offence and the sentence imposed on the applicant is in line with sentences imposed in similar cases. It was




argued that the applicant has no prospects of success both against conviction and sentence, and this application must be dismissed.


The law


  1. It is trite that a litigant seeking condonation bears an onus to satisfy the court that there is sufficient cause to warrant the grant of condonation. A litigant seeking condonation should launch such an application without delay. The factors to be considered in an application for the condonation of any failure to comply with the rules of the court are well established. They are expounded in several decisions of the courts and are well-established. They include the following: the extent of the delay; the reasonableness of the explanation for the delay or non-compliance; the prospects of success should the application be granted; the possible prejudice to the other party; the need for finality in litigation; the importance of the case; the convenience of the court; and the avoidance of unnecessary delays in the administration of justice. These factors are not exhaustive. See Read v Gardner & Anor 2019 (3) ZLR 575 (S); Forestry Commission v Moyo 1997 (1) ZLR 254 (S); Maheya v Independent African Church 2007 (2) ZLR 319 (S); Friendship v Cargo Carriers Ltd & Anor 2013 (1) ZLR 1 (S).


  1. In Melane v Santam Insurance Co Ltd 1962 (4) SA 531 (A), Holmes JA stated the following at para 532CF:


“In deciding whether a sufficient cause has been shown, the basic principle is that the Court has a discretion, to be exercised judicially upon a consideration of all the facts, and in essence it is a matter of fairness to both sides. Among the facts usually relevant are the degree of lateness, the explanation therefor, the prospects of success, and the importance of the case. Ordinarily, these facts are interrelated: they are not individually decisive, for that would be a piecemeal approach incompatible with true discretion, save, of course, that if there are no prospects of success, there would be no point in granting condonation. Any attempt to formulate a rule of thumb would only serve to harden the arteries of what should be a flexible discretion. What is needed is an objective conspectus of all the facts. Thus, a slight delay and a good explanation may help to compensate for the prospects of success, which are not strong. Or the importance of the issue and the strong prospects of success may tend to compensate for the long delay. And the Respondent’s interest in finality may not be overlooked. I would add




that discursiveness should be discouraged in canvassing the prospects of success in the affidavit. I think that all the foregoing clearly emerge from decisions of this Court, and therefore I need not add to the ever-growing burden of annotation by citing cases.”


  1. It is with these legal principles in mind that I will proceed to analyze the evidence presented and submissions made this application.


The application of the law to the facts


  1. Regarding the extent of the delay, the applicant was sentenced on 24 June 2022. In this context, r 102 (2) of the High Court Rules, 2021, provides that such an applicant must note his appeal within ten days of the passing of sentence. This application was filed on 29 September 2025. In essence, this application was launched approximately 3 years and 2 months outside the timeline allowed by the rules of court. By any stretch of the imagination, such a delay is inordinate.


  1. No doubt, a delay of over 3 years is substantial and requires a satisfactory explanation. As stated above, the applicant argued that he did not know of his right to appeal and had challenges in obtaining a transcribed record of proceedings. My view is that the explanation is just a façade designed to hoodwink this court. I say so because the applicant is not an unsophisticated person; he is a retired soldier. In addition, at his trial, he was represented by a legal practitioner. He cannot plead ignorance of his rights to note an appeal. Such a plea is not available to him. Further, the argument that he had no resources to pay for the transcribing of the record is a mere statement not supported by evidence. Without a supporting affidavit from his relatives explaining the steps taken, and the difficulties encountered; to pay for the record, I reject the applicant’s submission in this regard. It is merely contrived and meant to mislead. My conclusion is that there is no satisfactory explanation for the delay exceeding 3 years. He has just on reflection, decided to try his luck by launching this application.


  1. I now turn to the prospects of success on appeal. At the commencement of the hearing, the applicant informed the court that he was no longer persisting with his application in respect of the conviction. He, however, persisted with the application in respect of sentence arguing




that he has prospects of success on appeal against sentence. A reasonable prospect of success means that the court hearing the application for condonation must be satisfied that on the findings of fact and /or conclusions of law involved, the court of appeal may well take a different view from that arrived at by the trial court.


  1. In respect of conviction, the evidence against the applicant is overwhelming. He was positively identified by the first witness, Clyne Nyamande. In addition, in his evidence he located himself at the scene of the crime and explained the nature of the role he played in the commission of this offence. The evidence shows that he played an important role in disarming the security guard and used the gun to threaten to shoot Clyne Nyamande who was in possession of the bucket with the gold concentrate. He used the butt of the gun to assault one of the mine workers and pointed the gun at the mine workers. In view of his admissions and the totality of the evidence there is no doubt that the applicant committed this crime. He has no reasonable prospects of success on appeal against conviction. It is for these reasons that his abandonment of the application in respect of conviction was well thought out and well taken.


  1. As stated above, regarding the sentence, the applicant argued that he has prospects of success on appeal. He submitted that the sentence is so severe as to induce a deep sense of shock. He argued that the trial court did not pay regard to the fact that he was a first offender and misdirected itself in not considering suspending a portion of the sentence on condition of restitution. My view is that the applicant has no prospects of success on appeal against sentence. It is settled that sentencing lies within the discretion of the trial court, and an appellate court will only interfere with the sentence only if it is shown that there was a misdirection in principle or the sentence is disturbingly inappropriate. It is not enough for an appellant to argue that the sentence imposed is too severe because that alone is not a misdirection. See Mukutu Anor v The State S-05-26; S v Munakamwe S-121-23; S v Nhumwa S-40-88. Case law shows that the courts take a robust approach to the sentencing armed robbers. This is because armed robbery is a heinous offence, it usually involves cruelty, premeditation, criminal resolve, and purpose. It requires brazen execution. It is an attack on a human victim with the attendant disregard of that person’s right to personal security. It constitutes a forceful dispossession of the victim’s




property. For the victim, it is usually a terrifying, scary, horrifying, and degrading experience. It induces so much fear in the victims.


  1. In casu, the trial court factored into the sentencing equation that this robbery was well planned and executed. That in general robbery is a very serious offence, and that more so in this case, a firearm was used to subdue the victims. That the property stolen was of considerable value and was not recovered. The court also considered that the applicant used violence against the complainant’s employees, and he struck an employee with the butt of a gun. The robbers threatened the mine employees with death. The court concluded that the moral blameworthiness of the robbers was very high and that direct and severe imprisonment was warranted. I do not think that an appeal court can find fault with the sentence imposed on the applicant. In other words, it is very unlikely that an appeal court may, on the findings of fact and /or conclusions of law involved, take a different view from that arrived at by the trial court.


  1. In conclusion, the delay in seeking condonation has been inordinate, and no satisfactory explanation has been given for this substantial delay. In addition, the applicant has no prospects of success on appeal. In my view it will be devoid of logic to grant condonation in the circumstances of this case, particularly where the applicant does not enjoy good prospects of success on appeal. I am not persuaded that it is in the interests of justice to grant condonation sought by the applicant.


  1. For the aforesaid reasons and conclusions, the applicant failed, on a balance of probabilities, to make a case for the relief he sought in this court. It is for these reasons that this application was dismissed.
