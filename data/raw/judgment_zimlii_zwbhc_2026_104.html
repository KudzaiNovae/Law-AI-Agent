MPOKISENG DUBE J: The plaintiff’s claim is for:

  1. Payment of the sum of US$11 000.00 or its equivalent in Zimbabwean dollars using the prevailing bank rate, being the balance due to the Plaintiff.

  2. Interest thereof at the prescribed rate calculated from the date of issue of summons to date of final payment.

  3. Costs of suit.

The claim is entirely based on an acknowledgement of debt signed by the Defendant in favour of Plaintiff. Defendant herein resists payment on two fronts viz:

  1. That while admitting to signing the acknowledgment of debt, he did so under duress.

  2. That it is the plaintiff who voluntarily gave a sum of R11 500.00 for him (defendant) to buy gold for him for re-sale. Defendant claims to have been duped into buying fake gold. He then failed to return the cash. He then proposed that he and plaintiff work together at his mine so that the plaintiff can salvage the value of the lost cash. He believes Plaintiff did salvage the value and is not justified in making any further demands.

Brief background.

Plaintiff and defendant reside in the same neighbourhood. While Plaintiff is said to be a local businessman who owns two supermarkets, defendant’s profile is not very clear. He borders on being a small scale miner, a buyer of gold, a pastor and somehow a peddler in the fashionable business of faith based healing. What is striking, though is that plaintiff is an elderly man who is visually impaired while defendant is a healthy-looking 39-year-old at least at the time of his testimony.

Per plaintiff’s declaration, sometime in May 2020, the defendant borrowed the sum of US$15 000.00 from the plaintiff to boost his mining ventures at MMT mining syndicate. The defendant acknowledged his debt to the plaintiff in the sum of US$15 000.00 and to date he has only paid the sum of US$4 000.00, leaving a balance of US$11 000.00. Despite numerous demands, the defendant has failed, neglected and or refused to pay the balance of US$11 000.00.

In his plea, the defendant claims that the plaintiff used extortion and undue pressure on him, resulting in the signing of an acknowledgement of debt and an overpayment of the actual sum due.

Since the signing of a liquid document was common cause at the commencement of trial, I directed that the defendant testify first as the onus to prove duress put to bear on him.

The Law

The acknowledgement of debt attached and marked Exhibit “A” is in the form of a sworn declaration. In the document, the defendant admits to owing the plaintiff the sum of USD15 000.00. He undertakes to pay. He places a condition that favours himself to the effect that as soon as payment is made in full, he will remove plaintiff as a third Director at MTT Mine. He took an oath before an Officer in Charge Entumbane Police station, who is an ex-officio commissioner of oaths. Defendant does not deny his personal particulars as captured in that document. That can only mean he was the source of those particulars. He does not deny the signature affixed as his. He only contends that he did so under duress.

The law on what constitutes a liquid document is quite settled.

In Sibanda v Machaidze 2010 (1) ZLR 216 (H), the term liquid document was defined as follows:

“The term liquid document is not defined in the rules. This court has however held that any clear, unequivocal and unambiguous written promise to pay a debt constitutes a liquid document. Thus, any letter, to the extent that it is clear, unequivocal and unambiguous and contains an acknowledgment of debt, can constitute a liquid document for the purposes of the rules on provisional sentence.”

I find this legal proposition apt in the present case. The sworn declaration by the defendant is clear, unequivocal and unambiguous. It contains a promise to pay under oath. It thus qualifies squarely as a liquid document. Now the question is can this unequivocal acknowledgment of debt be vitiated by duress?

The elements necessary to set aside an acknowledgement of Debt on the grounds of duress were described by Corbett J, in Ared & another v Astra furnishers (PTY) LTD 1974 (1)SA 298 (C) as follows:

“It is clear that a contract may be vitiated by duress (metus), the raison d'etre of the rule apparently being that intimidation or improper pressure renders the consent of the party subjected to duress no true consent............duress may take the form of inflicting physical violence upon the person of a contracting party or inducing on him a fear by means of threats. Where a person seeks to set aside a contract, or resist the enforcement of a contract on the ground of duress based on fear, the following elements must be established:

  1. The fear must be a reasonable one.

  2. It must be caused by the threat of some considerable evil to the person concerned or their family

  3. It must be a threat of imminent or inevitable evil.

  4. The threat or intimidation must be unlawful and contra bonos mores

  5. The moral pressure use must have caused damage”

To determine this legal question, one has to closely look at the facts of the matter. Firstly the defendant introduces himself as a pastor, a spiritual leader in other words. As to who ordained him to the calling is not for this court to determine. However, their relationship of borrower and lender arises when defendant was in a vantage position of a pseudo saviour while plaintiff, as part of the flock desperately sought to have his sight restored.

Defendant claims in fact the cash regardless, of the amount, was for purposes of buying gold. When a man of the cloth dabbles in gold someone is bound to squeal. Unfortunately, this time around Defendant claims to have been the victim. He claims to have been duped into buying fake gold. I am not convinced with this narrative. 1st defendant does not mention the promise to restore plaintiff’s sight. However, when it is brought up he does not deny it. He introduces the subject of sprucing up his ailing mining business. Despite owing the plaintiff he instead blames him for failing to bring up investors nor a solid capital injection. How could that be if plaintiff joined the venture for the simple purpose of recovering his ZAR 11500.00 according to defendant? I am not convinced by defendant’s narrative. If anything he seemed to have wormed his way into plaintiff’s trust through a false promise of miraculously restoring plaintiff’s sight.

When it comes to the claims of duress, defendant’s narration does not add up. He claims to have been assaulted several times. In fact his mother says he was kidnapped thrice and robbed twice albeit in her absence. Defendant himself does not testify to that. His mother claims she and her husband were kidnapped. The incident involves them being forcibly bundled into plaintiff’s motor vehicle. While inside they are asked of defendant’s whereabouts. Her husbands asks an obvious question “Don’t you have his number to call him?” That question, immediately earns them their liberty. Surely could plaintiff have driven all the way to be asked an obvious question, which appeases him without even an answer? I do not think so. After that none of them lays charges at any police station.

The following morning defendant pops up. He tells his parents that they are required by Plaintiff at his shop. They flock there without protest. While there, a very civil meeting is held win which they freely participate. In the meeting, defendant’s father is addressed by plaintiff as “Father” In that meeting, defendant denies owing plaintiff and nothing happens to him. Instead, he protests that plaintiff took his machines, damaged his compressor, stole a truckload of gold ore, processed it, earned 30 grams of gold, and squandered the proceeds. Per that witness, Plaintiff says and does nothing. According to this witness in the presence of two burly “bouncers” or “comrades” as Defendant puts it. Plaintiff simply “suggests” that the Defendant deposes to an affidavit. After that the meeting is called off everyone goes home.

The following morning Defendant and his entourage again visits Plaintiff’s shop, of their own accord. Only then did Defendant “suggest” to pay the money demanded i.e USD15 000.00. He thereafter voluntarily pays up USD4000.00.

I must say the above does not paint a picture of a ruthless loan shark, who wants to extract every coin from the hapless borrower. I thus reject the assertion by Defendant that he never owed the sum claimed and that he attested to a sworn acknowledgment of debt under duress.

I am of the respectful view that the actions of the defendant in casu are not indicative of someone who will stop at nothing to avoid paying a debt. It is for that reason that I make the following disposition:

Disposition

The plaintiff’s claim succeeds, and the following order is made:

(a) Defendant to pay the sum of US$11 00.00 (Eleven thousand United States dollars) or the equivalent payable in Zimbabwean dollars (ZWL$) at the prevailing interbank rate on the day of payment.

(b) Interest on the said sum of US$11000.00 (Eleven thousand United States dollars), or the equivalent payable in Zimbabwean dollars (ZWL$) at the prevailing interbank rate on the day of payment, at the prescribed rate of 5% calculated from the date of Summons.

(c) Costs of suit on an attorney and client scale.
