MURASI J.,

INTRODUCTION

The Applicant was employed by the First Respondent. Allegations of misconduct were levelled against her and she was brought before a Disciplinary Committee. The Disciplinary Committee found her guilty and recommended her dismissal. Applicant appealed to the Second Respondent. This did not yield the desired result and Applicant approached this Court for relief in the form of an application for review.

On 27 October 2025, this Court issued an Order in Case Number LC/H/837/25 striking the matter off the roll as the application for review was deemed to be defective. Applicant is disgruntled with this outcome and intends to approach the Supreme Court on appeal. This is therefore an application for leave to appeal in terms of section 92 F (2) of the Labour Act (Chapter 28:01) as read with Rule 43 of the Labour Court Rules, 2017. The prospective grounds of appeal are formulated as follows:

  1. The court a quo misdirected itself and erred at law in raising, mero motu, and determining the question as to whether or not the Appellant’s grounds of review were concise and precise when such issue did not arise from the pleadings filed by the parties and was never raised by the 1st Respondent.

  2. The court a quo misdirected itself on a point of law in placing reliance on a case authority which was distinguishable from the one before it.

  3. Furthermore, the court a quo misdirected itself and erred on a point of law when it held that the Appellant’s application for review was not concise and precise within the contemplation of the law.

  4. The court a quo erred and misdirected itself in seeking to dispose of the matter on the basis of a legal technicality and despite the 11st Respondent’s admission of the irregularity which formed the basis of the Appellant’s first ground of review.

  5. The court a quo erred and misdirected itself in seeking to dispose of the matter on the basis of only one ground when the application was predicated on two grounds of review.

  6. It was grossly irregular for the court a quo to express reservations on the Appellant’s Second ground of review without hearing and considering argument on it.

PRELIMINARY POINTS

At the commencement of the hearing, Mr. Madzvamuse stated that he had preliminary points to raise. He submitted that the present application was frivolous and vexatious in that Applicant intended to attack the decision of the Court when such decision was not available to both the First Respondent and the Court. He further added that a decision by the Court a quo was not made part of the record as is customary. He argued that the Applicant wanted the Court to ‘speculate’ on what the reasons were which had led to the granting of the Order. He further pointed out that the Respondents were indeed prejudiced by Applicant’s actions in not attaching the reasons given by the Court. In this vein, Mr. Madzvamuse submitted that First Respondent had been unable to fully address the individual grounds of appeal filed because of the absence of the Court’s reasons. He further added that it was easy for the Applicant to request for those reasons or have the written Ex Tempore judgment typed.

Mr. Madzamuse stated that the attachment of reasons to the present application would enable the court to assess the prospects of success on appeal and in this case the Applicant had simply made averments which could not be verified on the record. He added that in the present circumstances, without the aid of reasons, Applicant had her own version of events whilst First Respondent’s version was different and this clearly created a serious dilemma for the Court. He pointed out that in the circumstances, the application was based on nothing and therefore was without foundation. First Respondent’s heads of argument contain the following in paragraph 7:

“It is the 1st Respondent’\s considered submission therefore that the absence of written reasons for judgment filed simultaneously with the application for leave to appeal, deals the final and fatal blow to this Application. This is so because it derogates the right of the Court to perceive any prospects of success from the intended appeal. The question that has to mostly exercise the mind of this Honourable Court is, how can this Court embark on any inquiry into the prospects of success of the intended appeal where there is no written reasons for judgment which the Applicant intends to impugn on appeal? The whole inquiry as to whether there are prospects of success or not is wholly derived from the written judgment.”

Mr. Madzvamuse prayed for the application to be struck off the roll with costs.

In response, Mr. Muchandiona submitted that Applicant’s application complied with the statutory requirements. He stated that section 92 F (2) and Rule 43 provided that the application be accompanied by a Draft Notice of Appeal and that it be made to the Judge who issued the judgment or order. He also submitted that the Chief Justice had directed that all courts subordinate to the Supreme Court were to give written Ex Tempore judgments for the orders issued from those courts. Asked by the Court as to why the record in casu did not have written reasons, he simply replied that these were read out in Court. Mr. Muchandiona insisted that he saw no defect in the application placed before the Court. Asked by the Court as to how the Court was to assess the prospects of success in the absence of written reasons for the Order, his response was that the Court could then refer to its notes. Again, the Court sought clarification as to how the Respondents were to deal with the application in the absence of the written reasons, he stated that the Respondents were present in the Court when the decision was delivered. The Court brought to Mr. Muchandiona’s attention the judgment from the South African Constitutional Court which dealt with the need for a written judgment to placed before an appellate court.

ANALYSIS

It is trite that a point in limine when raised should, firstly have merit and secondly, be likely to dispose of the matter. Put differently, a point in limine should have the requisite potency to decisively influence the outcome of the proceedings, that is, have a characterisation which is dispositive of the matter. (See generally Telecel Zimbabwe (Pvt) Ltd v Potraz & Ors HH 446/15; Warren Hills Golf Club v Sunshine Development Club HH 623/23).

The point raised by the First Respondent is clear and simple. It is to the effect that Applicant has not attached the reasons by the Court to enable the Court to come to a value judgment as to whether there are prospects of success on appeal. It is a truism that this Court has on several occasions in judgments from the Supreme Court been informed that it acts as a gate-keeper with the duty to sift and refer deserving cases on appeal to the Supreme Court. Apart from raising points of law, the prospective appeal has to have prospects of success. Evidently this balance has to be arrived at by having regard to both the intended grounds of appeal and the decision sought to be appealed against. Apart from that, First Respondent has clearly been prejudiced in that it cannot meaningfully respond to the grounds of appeal in the absence of the reasons given by the Court in its decision.

Elsewhere in this judgment it was observed that the Court brought to Applicant’s attention a decision of the South African Constitutional Court on this point. The relevant portions were read to Muchandiona in the proceedings. The decision was made in Strategic Liquor Services v Mvumbi T. N.O. & Ors [2009] ZACC 17 where it was stated as follows in paragraph 17 thereof:

“In Mphahlele, this Court noted that there is no express constitutional provision requiring the judges to furnish reasons for their decisions (and on this basis upheld the long-standing practice of the Supreme Court of Appeal not to furnish reasons when determining applications for leave to appeal). We add that there is likewise no express statutory provision requiring judges who have given judgment ex tempore to furnish written reasons when later required. Nonetheless, as this Court pointed out in Mphahlele, a reasoned judgment is indispensable to the appeal process. Judges ordinarily account for their decision by giving reasons- and the rule of law requires that they should not act arbitrarily and that they be accountable. Furnishing reasons-

‘explains to the parties, and to the public at large which has an interest in courts being open and transparent, why a case is decided as it is. It is a discipline which curbs arbitrary judicial decisions. Then, too, it is essential for the appeal process, enabling the losing party to take an informed decision as to whether or not to appeal or, where necessary, seek leave to appeal. It assists the appeal Court to decide whether or not the order of the lower court is correct. And finally, it provides guidance to the public in respect of similar matters.’”

The above sentiments from the apex court in that jurisdiction clearly points to the need for written reasons in the appeal process. The Court made the observation that after the relevant portions of the judgment were read out during Mr. Muchandiona’s submissions, he did not make any comment on the case. The above cited case highlights the importance of written reasons in the determination of an appeal. The cited case is persuasive in this jurisdiction. Mr. Muchandiona was unable to demonstrate how an appellate court would be able to resolve such a matter in the circumstances. It cannot be argued that only the prospective grounds of appeal and the court order would be enough for the exercise of determining whether there are prospects of success on appeal. This would be wellnigh impossible. It is my view that diligent research and consultation by Applicant’s legal practitioners would have led to the correct procedure being adopted. Indeed, it was easy for the Applicant’s legal practitioners to request for a typed copy of the Ex Tempore decision which was available on record. Alas, Applicant’s legal practitioners did not believe that this route was necessary.

It is also common cause that what Applicant intends to have placed before the Supreme Court falls into the category of ‘unterminated proceedings.’ The present matter was not heard on the merits. This issue was dealt with in FBC Bank Limited v Kudzai Kwangwari & Anor SC 17/25. UCHENA JA had this to say at pages 7-8 of the cyclostyled judgment:

“The applicant applied for leave to appeal against the striking off, of its application off the roll. This means its application for rescission of the default judgment has not been determined on the merits by the Court a quo. During the hearing Mr. Dube for the applicant, in response to my question on whether the applicant did not have a right to make a valid application for rescission of judgment to the court a quo said it could rely on that option.

It is therefore obvious that this is a case where the applicant chose to seek leave to appeal to this court instead of making a valid application for rescission of judgment, to the court a quo.

The application for rescission of judgment, has not been determined on the merits. It is trite that superior courts cannot lightly interfere with unterminated proceedings of lower courts

…

…

In the case of Prosecutor General of Zimbabwe v Intratek (Pvt) Ltd & Ors SC 67/20 MAKARAU JA (as she then was) dealing with the same issue at p 8 of the cyclostyled judgment said:

‘Thus, put conversely, the general rule is that superior courts must wait for the completion of the proceedings in the lower court before interfering with any interlocutory decision made during the proceedings. The exception to the rule is that only in rare or exceptional circumstances where the gross irregularity complained of goes to the root of the proceedings, vitiating the proceedings irreparably, may superior courts interfere with ongoing proceedings.'"

Applicant’s case is similar to the FBC Bank case supra where the matter had been struck off the roll. The rationale being that the litigant is still allowed to ‘correct’ the defect and approach the court for determination of the matter on the merits. As stated by UCHENA JA, this is also in line with Practice Direction 3 of 2013 where a party has thirty days in which to approach the court with the ‘cured’ pleadings.

The points in limine are meritorious.

The following order is appropriate.

  1. The point in limine is hereby upheld.

  2. The application for leave to appeal to the Supreme Court is hereby struck off the roll.

  3. Applicant to meet First Respondent’s costs on the ordinary scale.

  4. Applicant shall not institute proceedings against the Respondents, in this Court, on the same cause of action, without complying with paragraph 3 above or filing evidence of waiver from the Respondents for such non-compliance.
