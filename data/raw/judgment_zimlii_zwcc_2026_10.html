Introduction

  1. The applicant and the first respondent are not strangers to this Court. The litigation between them has been ongoing for more than twenty years. Part of that litigation has played out before this Court.


  1. On 4 November 2025, the parties appeared before the Supreme Court (the court a quo) in a contested appeal. The applicant was the appellant. Finding the appeal fatally defective, that court struck it off the roll with costs. Dissatisfied with the order, the applicant approached this Court seeking direct access. If successful, she intends to file a substantive application under s 85(1) (a) of the Constitution of Zimbabwe, 2013, alleging that the court a quo infringed her rights to equal protection and the benefit of the law as envisaged in s 56(1) of the Constitution, and to a fair hearing as provided for in s 69(2) and (3).

  2. The application for direct access is improperly before this court. No reasons for the order made a quo dismissing the application for leave to amend the draft order have been attached to the application.


  1. The application is a disguised and therefore impermissible appeal against the order a quo striking her appeal off the roll. The court a quo was perfectly within its rights to control the proceedings before it. Its decision in that regard is final and is not reviewable.


  1. In the ultimate analysis, the applicant’s approach to this Court at this stage of the proceedings, the allegations she makes against the court a quo, the language she employs in the pleadings, and the relief she seeks, all weighed together, constitute an abuse of the process of this Court warranting an award of costs. For the full reasons set out below, the application for direct access is struck off the roll with costs.


Background Facts

  1. As indicated above, the applicant and the first respondent have been involved in protracted litigation over the years. The applicant puts the period at 23 years. However, the genesis of the present application is more recent. It started in August 2024 when the first respondent issued a summons in the High Court under case number HCH 3766/24, alleging that the applicant had fraudulently registered half of his immovable property into her name. He approached the High Court seeking cancellation of the deed of transfer in the appellant’s favour.

  2. The claim was not defended, resulting in a default judgment being entered in his favour. The default judgment was, in due course, rescinded at the instance of the applicant by yet another default judgment.


  1. Aggrieved by the rescission, the first respondent lodged his own application for rescission. The High Court ruled against the applicant. In doing so, it dismissed the preliminary objections raised by the applicant, found that the first respondent gave a reasonable explanation for his failure to defend the matter, and that the appellant had effectively “snatched” at the judgment through improper service. Noting the suspicious circumstances surrounding the property transfer, the High Court ruled that the first respondent deserved to be heard on the merits.


  1. Unhappy with the decision of the High Court, the applicant noted an appeal to the court a quo on a number of grounds which are not relevant to the determination of this application. In opposing the appeal the first respondent raised a preliminary point. He observed that the relief sought in the appeal was fatally defective.


  1. A day before the hearing of the appeal, the applicant filed an application to amend her papers. At the hearing of the appeal, the court a quo dismissed the application to amend the draft order and struck the appeal off the roll. The reasons for dismissing the application to amend the notice of appeal have not been attached to the application.




The application

  1. Following the order a quo, the applicant approached this Court seeking direct access on the basis that I have set out above. The application was opposed.

  2. For expediency, in giving details of the application, I will combine the averments made in the application itself and in the founding affidavit, as well as the arguments made in the applicant’s heads of argument. The applicant did not make any oral submissions at the hearing of the matter, being content to abide by the heads of argument that she had filed.


  1. In her application and while in essence the applicant seeks to vindicate two of her rights allegedly violated by the court a quo, she starts off her tirade against that court by alarmingly characterising the order striking her matter off the roll as one “representing a fundamental departure from constitutional principles and the rule of law itself”.


  1. She further proclaims that “when a court departs from administering justice fairly and impartially, a sacred duty, whether through inconsistent application of rules, arbitrary decision making, or discriminatory treatment, it ceases to exercise judicial power legitimately. Its judgment does not merely constitute an error; it represents an abdication of the judicial function itself,” an allegation she makes against the court a quo when it struck her matter off the roll because her draft order was defective.


  1. Still maintaining her general attack on the court a quo, the applicant alleges in her application that the court a quo is part of the legal system that appears determined to favour the first respondent at each turn and, in the process, deny her justice. She avers that she endured sexual harassment at her workplace and, rather than receiving support and justice, she has been subjected to a legal ordeal that has compounded her trauma and has denied her the protection that the law promises all citizens. She has since found herself defending herself not only from her harasser but from a legal system, of which the court a quo is part, which is bent on denying her justice.


  1. Amidst the general attack against the court a quo, the applicant makes the specific allegation that the court a quo violated her right to equality and equal protection of the law as provided for in s 56(1) of the Constitution. The court allegedly condoned departures from its rules selectively in favour of the first respondent in 2022, while refusing to exercise the same discretion in her favour three years later when it struck her matter off the roll. The applicant concludes that this is “a stunning display of unequal treatment”, which violates her constitutional right to equality. She further proclaims that the decision to strike her matter off the roll was not only arbitrary but was capricious, given that three years prior, the court had condoned the first respondent’s non- compliance with its rules.


  1. As further evidence of the unequal treatment meted out to her by the court a quo, the applicant avers that in the three cases that were heard by the court on the day, she was the only litigant against whom an order of costs was made. She does not give the details of the other two cases, and neither does she attach the records of these cases to the application.


  1. Further, the applicant avers that her right to a fair hearing as enshrined in s 69(1) of the Constitution was violated as the court a quo struck her matter off the roll on account of the defect in the notice of appeal and thereby denied her the right to be heard on the merits of the appeal. In this regard, I cite the applicant's written argument where she argued:

“Section 69 guarantees the right to a fair hearing. The Applicant was denied this right when:


• Her appeal was struck off for a correctable defect

• The Supreme Court refused to exercise discretion it had previously exercised in favour of the First Respondent in Case No SC79/22.

• She sought amendment pursuant to Rule 48—it was refused

• Seven substantive grounds of appeal went unheard

• She filed heads of argument and points in limine—they were not considered because

the matter was struck off”. (the underlining is mine).


  1. The above narration constitutes the sum total of the applicant’s case against the court a quo and the basis upon which she seeks an order for direct access to this Court to file a s 85 (1) application vindicating her rights to equality and the protection and benefit of the law and the right to be heard.


  1. As indicated above, the application was opposed. However, it is not necessary that I set out the details of the opposition.


  1. The first respondent opposed the application on three grounds. Firstly, he denied the alleged discrimination, stating that the applicant’s appeal was defective and was properly struck off the roll. He argued, as a second defence, that the applicant has other remedies under Supreme Court rules and should not bypass these by prematurely invoking constitutional remedies. Finally, the applicant argued that the applicant is abusing the constitutional process to bring a “back door appeal.” He accordingly prayed for the application to be dismissed with costs.


The Law

  1. The principles governing direct access to this Court are well established. Not every person raising a constitutional issue is automatically entitled to approach this Court directly. Direct access is a discretionary remedy, granted only where the applicant has strictly complied with the requirements prescribed in this Court’s rules and has demonstrated that it is in the interests of justice that such an approach be granted. The applicable procedural requirements for applications of this nature are set out in r 26(4) of this Court’s rules.


  1. In cases where a Supreme Court decision is impugned as the basis for an application seeking direct access, the decisions in Lytton Investments (Private) Limited v Standard Chartered Bank Zimbabwe 2018(2) ZLR 743 (CC) and Zimbabwe Consolidated Diamond Company (Pvt) Ltd v Adelcraft Investments (Pvt) Ltd CCZ 2-24 to name a few, provide the governing framework for determining the reviewability of Supreme Court decision.



  1. The settled position is that decisions of the Supreme Court on non-constitutional matters are final. Such decisions may only be challenged before this Court where it is shown that the Supreme Court failed to act in accordance with the law in a manner that resulted in the infringement of a litigant’s rights.


  1. Accordingly, this Court’s jurisdiction is only engaged, and direct access may only be granted, where an applicant alleging an infringement of rights by the Supreme Court demonstrates how that Court acted contrary to the law in a manner that resulted in the infringement of the applicant’s rights.




  1. In casu, the court a quo, in controlling the proceedings before it, declared the relief sought by the applicant incompetent. This, the court was entitled to do. It is granted inherent powers to exercise this discretion by the Constitution itself. The reasonableness or otherwise of the exercise of that discretion cannot be assessed, as the reasons why the court a quo came to this conclusion were not placed before this Court. The applicant did not attach the reasons for the order. She merely attacks the discretionary exercise of jurisdiction as arbitrary and capricious.


  1. Thus, the applicant fails to clear the first hurdle in her quest for direct access. By failing to attach the reasons for the order a quo, she has not demonstrated that the court a quo failed to act in accordance with the law in any way. This is the first requirement set out in the authorities cited above. Having failed in this regard, it is unnecessary to proceed to the next leg of the test and discuss whether the decision a quo violated the applicant’s rights as alleged or at all.

  2. In addition to the above and in any event, the applicant is not entitled to the relief she seeks on another basis. It is also the settled approach of this Court to withhold its jurisdiction where effective redress may be obtained through other available remedies. In such circumstances, the doctrine of subsidiarity will be invoked to delay the jurisdiction of the Court.

  3. The Constitutional Court Act [Chapter 07:22] is also instructive regarding the application of the doctrine of subsidiarity in constitutional litigation. Section 23 of the Constitutional Court Act provides that:

“23 Conflict of constitutional and statutory remedies

The Court may decline to exercise its powers in relation to any claim for redress founded upon the contravention of the Constitution if it is satisfied that adequate means of redress for the contravention alleged are or have been available to the person concerned under other provisions of the Constitution or under any other law.”


  1. The import of the above provision has been the established practice of this Court. In Stone & Anor v Central Africa Building Society & Ors CCZ 05-24 at p. 18 paragraph 51, it was held as follows:

“It is a trite principle that the doctrine of subsidiarity serves as a gate keeping function to guard against the hearing of constitutional matters on an ad hoc basis. It ensures that the courts apply a general principle to all cases and that in that manner the courts are protected from criticism with regard to their decisions on which matters they consider as appropriate to determine. The principle thus ensures that there is certainty in the law and that the principle of constitutional consistency and validity required by the Constitution in the law is upheld. See Magurure & Ors v Cargo Carriers International (Pvt) Ltd (supra) and Berry (nee Ncube) & Anor v Chief Immigration Officer & Anor 2016(1) ZLR 38 (CC).

See also Zinyemba v Minister of Lands and Rural Resettlement & Anor 2016 (1) ZLR (23) CC, at 274F.


  1. Therefore, where redress can be found in other lower courts, this Court will withhold its jurisdiction, and direct access will be denied. See Chawira & Ors v Minister of Justice, Legal and Parliamentary Affairs CCZ 3-17.


  1. The applicant has a myriad of alternative remedies available to her and can obtain redress without resorting to constitutional relief. Put differently, there are many procedures available to the applicant by which she can side-step or by-pass the order striking her matter off the roll and continue with the litigation between her and the respondents.


Disposition

  1. This judgment centres on the issue of when costs may be awarded against a party in a constitutional matter and not on the merits of the application.


  1. It is my conclusion that the applicant misconstrues the content of her rights to equality, the protection and the benefit of the law and the right to a fair hearing. The order a quo violated neither of these rights, as the order was competently made by the court a quo in the exercise of its inherent powers to control its own proceedings after it had refused an application to amend the notice of appeal. The reasons for refusing the amendment are not on record, and therefore, the reasonableness or otherwise of such a decision cannot be assessed in favour of the application.


  1. The application for direct access was not only improperly filed, but was made in inflammatory language. At each turn, the applicant never missed an opportunity to attack the court a quo. Gratuitously, the court has been vilified for abdicating its authority, for being uneven-handed in the exercise of its discretionary jurisdiction and for being openly biased in favour of the first respondent. This attack arises from the fact that the applicant was not successful before the court a quo in applying to amend her notice of appeal. She exhibits the temperament of a bad loser who will not take no for an answer. Whilst the courts are not overly sensitive to criticisms of their decisions, the attack on a court must be measured and respectful. The applicant’s attack on the court a quo was neither. It is my further conclusion that she abused the process of the Court and used the application to mount a relentless and undeserved attack on the court a quo.


  1. After making a relentless attack on the court a quo, the applicant seeks an order directing that her matter be remitted to the court a quo before a different bench. As indicated above, she is unhappy with the decision a quo and seeks to have it set aside so that she can, as she says, “have her day in court.” She is not seeking a constitutional remedy.

  2. Not only did the applicant abuse the process of this court to mount an unwarranted attack on the court a quo, but she also abused the application to revisit the history and details of the decades-old litigation between herself and the first respondent. This was largely irrelevant in a matter where she alleged a violation of her rights by the Supreme Court in its judgment. She overburdened the Court with irrelevant material calculated to portray herself as a victim of her circumstances and of a legal system that is not coming to her aid.


  1. As observed above, the matter between the applicant and the respondents giving rise to this application has seen the rescission of two default judgments and is yet to be determined on the merits. Whilst the applicant is free to access this Court whenever her rights are threatened or violated, her approach to this Court at this stage of the proceedings not only delays the finalisation of the litigation, but more importantly, amounts to an abuse of the process of this Court, as she has other remedies at her disposal to move the litigation forward and hopefully to finality.




  1. Rule 63 of the Rules of the Constitutional Court, 2025 provides:

“63 (1) Generally, no costs are awarded in a constitutional matter: Provided that, in an appropriate case, the Court or the Judge, as the case may be, may make such order of costs as it or he or she deems fit.”



  1. The settled approach is that in constitutional matters, the Court is generally slow to make an award of costs, only doing so in exceptional circumstances.


  1. The approach was explained in the Liberal Democrats & Ors v President of the Republic of Zimbabwe E.D. Mnangagwa N.O. & Ors CCZ 7-18 judgment at p. 24 as follows:

“In the exercise of discretion, courts usually do not order costs against an unsuccessful private party who seeks to vindicate constitutional rights against the State or to protect and enforce the Constitution in the public interest. The rationale for the approach is that orders of costs in such circumstances might have a chilling effect on potential litigants in the same category as the unsuccessful litigant. As the approach is part of the exercise of discretion, a court may, in appropriate circumstances, order that an unsuccessful private party pay costs in a constitutional case.

The circumstances that may influence a court to exercise its discretion and order an unsuccessful private party to pay costs in a constitutional case include institution of frivolous or vexatious proceedings. Conduct in the proceedings is a factor a court is entitled to take into account in deciding whether to award costs against an unsuccessful litigant. The test is that the award of costs should be just when regard is had to the facts and circumstances of the case. It would not be in the interests of the administration of justice to encourage litigants to believe that they are free to institute constitutional proceedings challenging the constitutionality of State action on spurious grounds. Awards of costs against unsuccessful litigants, in appropriate constitutional litigation cases, are a necessary means for the protection of the integrity of the judicial process and maintenance of public confidence in it.”



  1. The same approach appears to have been adopted in South Africa. In the case of Affordable Medicines Trust & Ors v Minister of Health & Ors 2006 (3) SA 247 (CC) at 296H-297E, it was stated that:

“The award of costs is a matter which is within the discretion of the Court considering the issues of costs. It is a discretion that must be exercised judicially having regard to all the relevant considerations. One such consideration is the general rule in constitutional litigation that an unsuccessful litigant ought not to be ordered to pay costs. The rationale for this rule is that an award of costs might have a chilling effect on the litigants who might wish to vindicate their constitutional rights, but this is not an inflexible rule. There may be circumstances that justify departure from this rule such as where the litigation is frivolous or vexatious. There may be conduct on the part of the litigant that deserves censure by the Court which may influence the Court to order an unsuccessful litigant to pay costs. The ultimate goal is to do that which is just having regard to the facts and circumstances of the case.”



  1. In addition to the above, and more relevantly, in the Liberal Democrats case (supra). At p. 27, the Court held that:

“The litigation amounted to abuse of court process. The applicants made malicious allegations of improper conduct against officials in the registry of the Constitutional Court, accusing them of colluding with state security agents to make documents relating to their application disappear. They knew that the allegations were false. They conducted themselves in this manner to attract publicity for political reasons.

Although the applicants are unsuccessful private parties in a constitutional case, their conduct justifies an award of costs against them.”



  1. In light of the foregoing, to protect the integrity of this Court's processes, I am inclined to depart from the Court's general position and award costs against the applicant as the unsuccessful party.


  1. In the result, I make the following order:

    1. The application is struck off the roll.

    2. The applicant is to pay the first respondent’s costs.
