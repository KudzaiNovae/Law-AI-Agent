MUCHAWA J: The accused person stands charged of contravening s 47 of the Criminal Law (Codification and Reform) Act, [Chapter 9:23]; being murder.

It is alleged that on 22 May 2021 and at house number 14449 Damafalls, Ruwa, the accused with intent to kill or realizing that there was a real risk or possibility that his conduct might cause death but continued in that conduct all the same, assaulted Tinotenda Mandiyanike with a baton stick, electric cables and a wheel spanner all over the body thereby causing injuries from which Tinotenda Mandiyanike died.

Summary of the State Case

The accused together with Masimba Makoto and Wallen Mupunga, both of whom were tried and convicted separately and already sentenced to life imprisonment, are said to have assaulted the deceased. They had confronted the deceased and one Stanley Kahari accusing them of having stolen some drugs which included crystal meth, seven pairs of jean trousers and cash amounting to US$300 from Masimba Makoto’s house. The assault was aimed at eliciting a confession. They were assaulted using electric cables, a baton stick and car jumpers. Burning plastics were also dropped on the deceased and Stanley Kahari. At some point the deceased was bundled into Masimba Makoto’s car and driven to his home to recover the money. Such money was not found despite searches in the presence of the deceased’s sister Vivienne Mandiyanike and Mirriam Mangozho, a housemaid.

Thereafter the deceased was taken to Sunway City and the assault continued. Members of the public who tried to respond to the deceased’s cry for help were threatened. It was discovered that the deceased was unconscious and the accused persons drove to Windsor 24 Hour Clinic where a doctor confirmed that the deceased had died and referred them to the police station to make a report. They did not do that but went back to the deceased’s house and advised them that the deceased had escaped and they were searching for him.

At night they drove past the tollgate in Norton, along the Harare-Bulawayo Road and dumped the deceased’s body whilst staging an accident scene. Indeed around 2200 hours ZRP Norton Traffic received a report of a road traffic accident and attended the scene and lodged the deceased’s body at Norton Hospital mortuary.

Investigations later led to the arrest of Wallen Mupunga on 30 May 2021. Wallen Mupunga implicated Masimba Makoto who then handed himself to the police on 11 August 2021.

The accused was only arrested on 16 May 2024.

A post mortem examination was conducted on the body of the deceased by Doctor Yoandry Olay Mayedo. He concluded that death was due to (a) bepoliet occipital subdural hematoma (b) severe head trauma.

  1. That the deceased and accused persons were friends who were working for Masimba Makoto an already convicted accomplice

  2. The deceased was suspected of having stolen some cash, drugs and jeans from Masimba Makoto.

  3. That the accused person and his convicted accomplices assaulted the deceased for a protracted period leading to, inter alia, severe head trauma and bipolet occipital subdural hematoma. This was the cause of the deceased’s death.

  5. Masimba Makoto was aged 38 years whilst the accused was aged 24 years at the time of the offence.

Disputed Facts

The deciding disputed fact is whether the accused was compelled by Masimba Makoto to assault the deceased.

The defence of compulsion is provided for as a complete defence in s 243 and 244 of the Criminal Law (Codification and Reform) Act [Chapter 9:23]. Compulsion is given as a complete defence in section 243 and section 244 contains additional requirements where one is facing a charge of murder.

To be a complete defence, the compulsion must consist of a threat unlawfully to kill the accused or some other person immediately if the accused did not kill or assist killing the deceased. The accused must show that he could not escape from or resist the threat referred to and the accused had no warning of the threat referred to, to enable him to forestall it, whether by reporting the matter to the police or by other means. In addition, the accused person should satisfy the requirements in section 243 (1) (b), (c) and (d). It must be shown that he or she believed on reasonable grounds that the implementation of the threat had begun or was imminent. The threat must also not be brought about through his own fault and he or she must have believed that on reasonable grounds that she/he could not escape or resist from the threat and that his or her conduct was necessary to avert the implementation of the threat.

The first issue to be decided is whether Masimba Makoto gave out a threat unlawfully to kill the accused if he did not assist in killing the deceased.

Masimba Makoto outrightly denied this. As the principal perpetrator, his accomplice evidence has to be treated with caution in terms of s 267 of the Criminal Procedure and Evidence Act [Chapter 9:07]. In the case of Elphas Ncube v The State SC 33/2016 Gowora JA (as she then was) had this to say about accomplice evidence:

“The reason for the existence of the cautionary rule regarding the evidence of accomplice witnesses in criminal trials is trite. An accomplice is a self-confessed criminal, and various considerations may lead him to falsely implicate an accused person, such as the desire to shield the culprit, or the hope of clemency where he has not yet been sentenced. Additionally, by reason of his inside knowledge he has a deceptive facility for a convincing description of facts, his only fiction being the substitution of the accused for the real culprit.

 

The rule requires that the court should warn itself of the danger of convicting on the evidence of an accomplice. Having done so by contrasting the evidence of the accomplice with that of the accused and viewing it against all the surrounding circumstances and the general probabilities of the case, the court must be satisfied beyond a reasonable doubt that the danger of false incrimination has been eliminated. It is not enough for the trial court to merely warn itself of the dangers of false incrimination and then convict simply on its faith in the honesty of the accomplice witness, based on nothing more than his demeanor and the plausibility of his story.”

 

In applying the above test, we found Masimba Makoto’s evidence to be incredible or implausible. He did everything possible to deny any evidence which would show the depth of his unlawful acts in order to minimize his involvement in the commission of the offence. The following examples are illustrative.

  1. He denied that he was the accused’s employer when the evidence of Wallen Mupunga and the accused clearly established so.

  2. He denied that he was operating a drug den and that part of the stolen property included crystal meth when the evidence of the accused and Wallen Mupunga proved the contrary.

  3. Whereas Masimba Makoto’s defence in his own trial had been to initially distance himself from the assault on the deceased and claiming that it was the accused who single-handedly assaulted the deceased, he now claims that all three of them assaulted the deceased.

  4. Another outright lie was Masimba Makoto’s denial that the accused was staying at his house. This was refuted by the evidence of the accused and Wallen Mupunga that the accused stayed in one of the cars at Masimba Makoto’s house.

  5. Masimba Makoto insisted that the door to the room in which the assault occurred was unlocked contrary to the evidence of both the accused and Wallen Mupungu.

  6. Masimba Makoto denied having initially accused the accused person of being the thief despite the evidence of both Wallen Mupunga and the accused.

  7. He also denied that he assaulted the accused first before turning on the deceased and Stanley Kahari.

  8. Masimba Makoto lied that his son would use commuter omnibuses to go to school and was not driven there in a private vehicle by the accused, yet it was agreed that during this period public transport, including commuter omnibuses, were banned.

What Masimba Makoto did was to deny any form of control he may have wielded upon the accused. This is why he denied that he was his employer. He denied that the accused lived at his house. He lied that he had not assaulted the accused first on suspicion of being the thief. But as we saw, his method of dealing with suspected thieves was to assault them, as he did with the deceased. He even lied that the door was unlocked in order to give the impression that the accused had been free to leave but voluntarily associated in the assault on the deceased.

We found that Masimba Makoto was not a credible witness on several critical issues and rejected his testimony.

Wallen Mupunga’s evidence is also subject to the cautionary rule as he was also an accomplice. He gave conflicting evidence regarding whether the accused had been forced by Masimba Makoto to perpetrate the assault. When quizzed about his conflicting evidence he said that he was not disputing that his evidence was inconsistent. In one breath he said the accused was forced and would change to say he was not forced. He even qualified this to say that the deceased had been forced by circumstances which circumstances he did not elaborate. It appeared to us that Wallen Mupunga was torn between shielding Masimba Makoto and telling the truth.

We therefore had to contrast the evidence of the accomplices with that of the accused and view such evidence against all the surrounding circumstances and the general probability of the case. In a way, these are the circumstances Wallen Mupunga referred to.

The accused was 24 years old and economically dependent on Masimba Makoto during the Covid era. Masimba Makoto was around 39 years old. The accused was even living in a motor vehicle at Masimba Makoto’s house because that is what he prescribed for the kind of work they were doing, that is selling drugs. This is why the accused was at Masimba Makoto’s house at 12:00 midnight on the date in question.

As an employee of Masimba Makoto the accused was under his control.

Masimba Makoto’s methods of dealing with thieves was assaulting them. This is what he did to the deceased. He had suspected the accused of the theft first. It follows that his usual method had followed. An assault on the accused. This only stopped when he was reminded that he had spent the day with the accused. The door was locked as per Walen Mupunga and accused’s testimony.

The accused said that he was friends with the deceased whom he had been working closely with. They had grown up together and he had been a conductor in commuter omnibuses he drove. By then, due to the Covid restrictions, they were selling drugs for Masimba Makoto. Why would he voluntarily assault his friend? Masimba Makoto was seeking a confession regarding his stolen items. Wallen Mupunga wanted the stolen money recovered so he could be paid. There is no clear reason why the accused would voluntarily join in the assault.

We have no choice but to accept the accused’s testimony that Masimba Makoto who already wielded control over him had threatened him unlawfully to assist in assaulting the deceased.

The evidence that the door was locked and Masimba Makoto’s own evidence that he was sitting by the door hence making it impossible for the accused to flee shows that he could not escape from the threat.

The evidence shows that from around 7.00 pm to midnight the accused person and convicted accomplices had been drinking whiskey together and taking crystal meth. This means that the accused had no warning of the threat to enable him to forestall it.

The evidence that the accused was the first suspect to be assaulted for the theft and that he had been previously assaulted by use of the baton stick and knew that Masimba Makoto possessed a gun, shows that he had reasonable grounds to believe that implementation of the threat had begun or was imminent.

There is no basis to believe that the threat was brought about by the accused’s own fault. He had experienced firsthand Masimba Makoto’s ferocity as he assaulted him using a baton stick targeting the head area. He also saw how the deceased and Stanely Kahari were tied up, asked to remove their clothes and hit on the knees with a wheel spanner. He saw an iron bar tied to the legs to ensure no escape. The accused therefore had reasonable grounds to believe that he could not escape and his conduct was necessary to avert implementation of the threat.

Furthermore, the child locks on the car and the fact that Masimba Makoto was driving and in control of the events of the day confirms the accused’s version.

The accused person has satisfied all the requirements for the defence of compulsion as a complete defence to murder. In the circumstances of this case, he cannot be found to have voluntarily associated with the two convicted accomplices in perpetrating the assault which resulted in the deceased’s death.

We therefore find the accused not guilty of murder as defined in section 47 (1) of the Criminal Law (Codification and Reform) Act [Chapter 9:23] and acquit him.
