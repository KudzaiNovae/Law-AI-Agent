Remittal from the Supreme Court – Property Sharing



MAXWEL J:

The judgement in this matter was issued on 28 January 2025. In dealing with the parties’ immovable properties, paragraphs 7 and 8 awarded 50% to each of the parties in House no 463 off Portgen Pomona, Borrowdale and Stand Number 205 Mabvuku Shopping Centre, Mabvuku, Harare. Plaintiff was granted the sole right of occupation in the Borrowdale immovable property until the youngest child is 18 years old. Defendant was granted the sole right to run the business at Mabvuku Shopping Centre until the youngest child is 18 years old. After the youngest child reached 18 years, the properties were to be evaluated and the parties would have the option to buy each other out failing which the properties would be sold to the best advantage and the proceeds thereof shared equally between the parties.

Defendant was aggrieved and noted an appeal in the Supreme Court. His appeal under reference SC304/25 was partially successful. The part relevant to this judgement reads

“3. Paragraphs 7 and 8 of the judgement of the court a quo be and is hereby set aside.

4. The matter is remitted to the court a quo for it to determine an appropriate distribution of the parties’ immovable properties taking into account the provisions of Section 7 of the Matrimonial Causes Act, Cap 5:13.”


On the 24th of November 2025 a case Management Meeting was held and the parties agreed on filing supplementary submissions, which they did.

Section 7 of the Matrimonial Causes Act Cap 5:13 regulates the division, apportionment and distribution of assets of the spouses on dissolution of the marriage. Section 7(3) (c) provides that

“The power of an appropriate court to make an order in terms of paragraph (a) of subsection (1) shall not extend to any assets which are proved, to the satisfaction of the court, to have been acquired by a spouse, whether before or during the marriage

(a) ……………

(b) …………….

(c) In any manner and which have particular sentimental value to the spouse concerned.”


Defendant’s argument is that the Mabvuku property had sentimental value to him and had been acquired by him within the contemplation of section 7(3) (c) of the Matrimonial Causes Act Cap 5:13. In Supplementary submissions he bases his argument on the following aspects of his evidence.

“31.1 Defendant’s father started business operations in Mabvuku way back in 1966.

31.2 Defendant’s first business as an individual started in Mabvuku in 1998.

31.3 Over the past 26 years of doing business in Mabvuku, Defendant created personal attachment and association with the Mabvuku community.

31.4 The Mabvuku community together with the Defendant have memories during the food shortages period from 1998 to 2008 and period thereafter when Defendant worked throughout to bring food supplies for Mabvuku Community at correct prices.

31.5 Defendant values Mabvuku shop more than monetary value.

31.6 Defendant managed to purchase Mabvuku shop through the community recommendation to City of Harare as reflected by the Agreement of Sale (Exhibit B page 431 to 433 of Record).

31.7 Defendant got that stand from the community on the basis that Defendant had future and current plans in place to bring a world class shopping experience for the Mabvuku community as promised when he got that stand.

31.8 The Defendant describes “attachment with the community” and serving them “wholeheartedly” creating a strong emotional bond beyond financial benefit.

31.9 Family and History: The long standing family business in Mabvuku since 1966.

31.10 The Defendant’s own business from 1998 built a legacy and deep roots in that specific location”


Plaintiff disputed that the Mabvuku property had sentimental value to the Defendant. She argued that the property was acquired by both parties during the subsistence of their marriage and not by the defendant alone. She stated that sentimental value must attach to the acquisition of the property. She averred that when the defendant was serving the community she was already there and they bought the property together, over ten years into their marriage of equals. Plaintiff pointed out that when the parties got married they both had nothing. In the course of their marriage they started acquiring assets together from proceeds of business they had established.

In Diya v Diya HH 3/24, I relied on the online Dictionary’s definition of sentimental value. It is defined as

“the value of an object deriving from personal or emotional associations and memories rather than material worth.”


Section 7 (3) (c) of the matrimonial causes Act Cap 5:13 is specific that the power of an appropriate court to distribute, divide or apportion the assets of the spouses shall not extend to assets acquired before or during the marriage in any manner and which have particular sentimental value to the spouse concerned. The question that arises therefore is whether the manner in which the Mabvuku property was acquired clothes it with sentimental value. Plaintiff testified that both parties funded the acquisition of the shop. She was asked.

“Q. Did the Defendant inject some separate money for this growth or the money was coming from the very first shop together with the other shops as you were growing?”


Her answer was;

“A. It was money from the first shop that was injected into other shops. It was the money that came out from the business that we had that we used. It was money from the business that bought trucks and also the cars that we were now using.”

(page 14 of the transcript).


Defendant does not dispute that the Mabvuku stand was acquired using money from the business. The focus of Section 7(3) of the Matrimonial causes Act Cap 5:13 is on assets that are acquired by one spouse to the exclusion of the other. In my view, assets that are jointly acquired do not qualify for the exemption as both parties will have sentimental attachment to jointly acquired property. Defendant argued that even though the property was acquired during the subsistence of the marriage, the Plaintiff was not involved in the running of the business. The property was acquired seven years into the parties’ marriage. The following quotation in the case of Watchtel v Watchtel 1773 (1) ALLER 829 is apposite;

“In the generality of marriages the wife bears and rears children and minds the home. She thereby frees her husband for his economic activities. Since it is her performance of her function which enables the husband to perform his, she is in justice entitled to share in its fruits.”

In casu the fruits of the economic activities include the Mabvuku Property. The facts presented by the Defendant as justifying exemption in terms of Section 7 (3) of the Matrimonial Causes Act Cap 5:13, in my view, assist the court in deciding who will have the first option to buy the other out after sharing the property. He claims that his father started business operations in Mabvuku way back in 1966. He did not go further to say he inherited the business from his father. Neither did he claim to have used funds from his father’s business to set up his own. If anything, he was inspired by the memory of what his father had done in the past. He further said he started a business as an individual in Mabvuku in 1998. It is common cause that his business closed at one point and as stated above, when the Mabvuku stand was acquired. Plaintiff had been by his side for seven years. I am of the view that reminiscing about the past does not fall within the exemption afforded by Section 7 (3) of the Matrimonial Causes Act Cap 5:13.

Defendant’s interactions with, attachment to, and association with the Mabvuku community do not shield the property from being divided, apportioned or distributed. In my view all it entitles the defendant to is an opportunity to continue operating therein after Plaintiff has gotten her share. He can pursue his plans and vision for the business after the property has been shared. In the case of Mukanyima v Mukanyima HH87/200, Gowora J (as she then was) referred to the case of Wachtel v Wachtel 1973 (1) ALLER 829 in which Lord Denning MR described family assets as those things which are acquired by one or other or both of the parties, with the intention that they should be continuing provision for them and their children during their joint lives and used for the benefit of the family as a whole. In that case the learned Judge did not accept that a house meant for the benefit of the family had a sentimental value. It was considered as an investment just like the acquisition of the Matrimonial home. See also Kumirai v Kumirai HH 17/2006.

It is common cause that the parties and their children were sustained by proceeds from the Mabvuku Shop. None of the exclusionary qualities attach to it. I find that as the property was not inherited or acquired in terms of any customary law, it is not exempt from being shared. It was an investment made in the course of a marriage. I do not accept that it falls in the category of property exempted from sharing in terms of Section 7 (3) of the Matrimonial causes ACT Cap 5:13. The result is that there are two immovable properties available for distribution, apportioning and division. These are house number 463 off Portgen, Pomona, Borrowdale and Stand Number 205 Mabvuku Shopping Centre, Mabvuku, Harare.

Section 7(1) of the Matrimonial Causes Act [cap 5:13] regulates the division, apportionment and distribution of assets of the spouse, on dissolution of marriage. Section 7(4) of the same Act sets out factors which the court must have regard to in dividing, distributing and apportioning the assets of the spouses. These are;

“(a) the income earning capacity assets and other financial reserves which each spouse and child is likely to have in the foreseeable future;

(b) the financial needs, obligations and responsibilities which each spouse and child has or is likely to have in the foreseeable future;

(c) the standard of living of the family, including the manner in which any child was being educated or trained or expected to be educated or trained,

(d) the physical and mental condition of each spouse and child,

(e) the direct or indirect contribution made by each spouse to the family including contributions made by looking after the home and caring for the family and any other domestic duties , and

(f) the value to either of the spouses or to any child of any benefit, including a pension or gratuity, which such spouse or child will lose as a result of the dissolution of the marriage.

In Gonye v Gonye 2009 (1) ZLR 232, at 236 H 237 B Malaba JA (as he then was) remarked

“It is important to note that a court has an extremely wide discretion regarding the granting of an order for the division of the assets of the spouses in divorce proceedings. Section 7 (1) of the Act provides that the court may make an order with regard to the division, apportionment or distribution of the assets of the spouses including an order that any asset be transferred from one spouse to the other. The rights claimed by the spouses under section 7 (1) are dependent upon the exercise by the court of the broad discretion. It must however be added that the Court’s untied discretion can only be exercised on the basis of the evidence led by the parties. In exercising its wide discretion, a court must determine the proportions on which it intends to distribute the assets of the parties. It should thereafter rely on the ratio it will have allocated to him or her.”


Defendant, in his closing submissions heavily relies on the case of Chigwada v Chigwa & Ors SC 118/20 and argues that as the parties were married out of community of property, he has the freedom to dictate what happens to properly registered in his name. He proposed that the Plaintiff be awarded 25% in the Borrowdale property whilst he gets 75% and the Mabvuku property. Whilst the Chigwada case allows parties to a marriage out of community of property to own and dispose of property in their individual capacities, that freedom does not extend to property that is subject of divorce proceedings. As stated above, the Matrimonial Causes Act Cap 5:13 gives the court power to divide, distribute and apportion assets of the spouses, including making an order that any asset be transferred from one spouse to the other. In Watchel v Watchel (supra ) it is stated.

“If the court comes to the conclusion that the home has been acquired and maintained by the joint efforts of both, then when the marriage breaks down, it should be regarded as the joint property of both of them, no matter in whose name it stands. Just as the wife who makes substantial money contributions usually gets a share, so should the wife who looks after the home and cares for the family for 20 years or more.”


This position has been emphasized in our jurisdiction. In the case of Usayi v Usayi 2003 (1) ZLR 168 it is established that a wife’s indirect contribution cannot be quantified in monetary terms. This is more so in casu, where the marriage lasted for 20 years and four children were born out of it. The plaintiff testifies that the last two pregnancies were life threatening and she had complications which resulted in her uterus being removed. Though the defendant expressed disgruntlement with plaintiff as a mother, the fact that the marriage lasted for 20 years and produced four children cannot be overlooked. I am persuaded that this is a case in which the overarching principle of equality in sharing the assets of the spouses on dissolution of marriage is applicable. Moreover, defendant in his plea demonstrated an equal sharing approach. He proposed that the Borrowdale property be used to acquire three apartments to be registered in a family Trust and then in the event that there is a balance, it be distributed equally between the parties. He proposed that both partied be beneficiaries of the Family Trust to be created and that both nominate two professional individuals each to act as remunerated trustees until the minors attain the age of majority. The proposal for equal distributions of the balance is indicative of the fact that defendant accepts that plaintiff is entitled to 50% of the property. Defendant prayed for an order that the Borrowdale Property be not distributed until the youngest child turns 18 years. Defendant’s request will ensure that the standard of living of the minor children in the area of accommodation is maintained.

Defendant submitted that the Mabvuku property is his only source of income as the bread winner of the family. In her declaration, plaintiff prayed that the defendant pays all the children’s expenses as well as maintaining her. The children’s expenses and the spousal maintenance are obligations which Defendant has though both have a time span. It is in the interest of justice not to disturb the source of income until the obligations have been discharged. I therefore make the following order

  1. Each party be and is hereby awarded 50% of

  1. House number 463 off Portgen Pomona, Borrowdale Harare

  2. Stand number 205 Mabvuku shopping centre Mabvuku Harare

  1. Plaintiff is granted the sole right of occupation in House number 463 off Portgen, Pomona Borrowdale, Harare until the youngest child attains 18 years

  2. Defendant is granted the sole right to run the business on stand number 205 Mabvuku , Harare until the youngest child attains 18 years

  3. After the youngest child attains 18 years, the provisions stated in paragraphs 9-15 of HH 44/25 will apply
