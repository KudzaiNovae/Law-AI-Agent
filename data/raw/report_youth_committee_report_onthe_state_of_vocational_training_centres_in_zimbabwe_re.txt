1.0 Introduction
1.1 Youth participation is a critical cog in the political and socio-economic development discourse of the country. Section 20 of the Constitution of Zimbabwe exhorts institutions of government to take reasonable measures, including affirmative action programmes to ensure that youths have access to appropriate education, training, skills and opportunities for employment. The Zimstat 2024 Third Quarter labour-force survey report shows that 2.3 million (2 302 369) youths are not engaged in any employment activity, education or training. The current infrastructure can accommodate a maximum of about 12 000 youth per year, which is just a drop in the ocean considering the number of youth in need of training. There is need for adequate, conducive facilities and vocational training centres that cater for this delicate demographic group. 
1.2 In the same vein, the Zimbabwe National Development Strategy (NDS) 1 blueprint, recognizes the youth as “a valuable resource” which should be afforded the opportunity to build, develop and strengthen own qualities to facilitate growth and flourishment into responsible citizens. In order to equip the youth with the requisite training and skills in line with the country’s 2030 Vision, the government has established Vocational Training Centres (VTCs) to provide and develop entrepreneurial and self-reliant skills training in areas such as agriculture, hospitality and tourism, welding, motor mechanics, business studies, carpentry, building studies, clothing technology, cosmetology, and domestic electrical installation among the youth.
1.3 Considering the current state of affairs within vocational training institutions, the Committee conducted an enquiry into the state of operations of VTCs, the existing infrastructure and the syllabus being taught. This report contains the findings, observations and recommendations made by the Committee.
2.0 Objectives
The key objectives of the inquiry were to:
Assess the state of facilities and operations in vocational training centres including resource availability;
Assess the relevance of current curricula in meeting industry demands;
Identify challenges faced at the VTCs and proffer recommendations to the development of effective vocational training institutions.
3.0 Methodology
The Committee received oral evidence from the Ministry of Youth Empowerment, Development and Vocational Training which provided a detailed overview of the efforts, programmes and policies being implemented under vocational training in line with the National Development Strategy One (NDS 1). The Committee also conducted field visits to selected Vocational Training Centres across the country from 10 to 15 March, 2025. During the visits the Committee had first-hand appreciation of the state of the facilities, interacted with the principals, staff and students at the centres. 4.0 Committee’s Findings
4.1 Vocational Training Programme
The Vocational Training Programme in Zimbabwe was established as a strategic response to youth unemployment and skills shortages in the country mainly targeting out of school youth and aspiring entrepreneurs. The Vocational Training Programme aims to develop an entrepreneurial and self-reliant youths and communities through vocational skills and business management training, providing training for enterprise development programmes, life skills that enable trainees to establish their own businesses with practical and technical skills relevant to various trades such as carpentry, motor mechanics, agriculture, clothing technology, and building. The programme was designed to complement the formal education system by offering alternative pathways to economic participation and empowerment. Over the years, the programme has been providing skills to the youth but still has a huge gap to over in terms of the enrolment capacity, keeping up with modern courses to offer and also the modern infrastructure that complements industrial trends.
4.2 Current Status of VTCs
4.2.1 During the oral evidence engagements the Committee learnt that the Ministry of Youth Empowerment, Development and Vocational Training was mandated to spearhead the vocational training and skills training initiative. Through the department of Vocational Training and Skills Development, skills training programmes were being conducted through a network of 45 Vocational Training Centres (VTCs) and 25 satellite VTCs under the Training for Enterprise (TFE) flagship. The TFE programme was aimed at providing competence-based training which integrates entrepreneurship and business management. Vocational training programme largely targets the youth population comprising of those out of school, school leavers and aspiring entrepreneurs. The target youth niche for VTCs constitutes a population most affected by drug and substance abuse, thereby making vocational training and skills development a major component for effectively fighting the cancerous scourge of drug and substance abuse.
4.2.2 The current enrolment figures at VTCs across the country remain significantly lower than the projected demand for vocational skills training. The Committee gathered that ccurrently the national enrolment at VTC institutions is hovering around 9 500 students against a possible demand of 130 000. There is a clear gap between the demand in skills and the carrying capacity of the institutions and an urgent need for increased investment in vocational training to align with the national objective of promoting employment creation and economic productivity. Kaguvi VTC in Midlands currently had the highest enrolment of 1 000 students whereas VTCs in the Matebeleland region such as Umguza and Phangani had a combined enrolment of not more than 600 students and yet the region had a high demand of skills and training institutions.
4.2.3 The revitalization of VTCs across the country is critical in achieving Vision 2030 through skills development, innovation and industrialization. Most VTCs especially those situated outside urban centres such as Magamba, Mushagashe and Phangani VTCs, sit on a substantial land which calls for investment and support in agriculture in terms of modern farming tools and equipment. The majority of the VTCs are housed in old farm houses, former barracks, refugee camps, Council halls and old disused beerhalls which need a major facelift and retooling. Tools, equipment, machinery and irrigation infrastructure are mostly inadequate and obsolete.
4.2.4 There were some VTCs such as Kadoma VTC which was allocated 32 hectares of land to construct a multipurpose workshop in 2014 but there has not been significant development to date. The progress and pace of development on the land which was allocated is slow owing to financial challenges. The progress that had been made to date is not satisfactory and leaves a lot to be desired. There is need for deliberate capital injection to complete the programme considering the skills and training demand. The completion of structures at the provided sites would go a long way in the betterment of operations of VTCs and also the efficiency of services offered at VTCs.
4.3 Courses on offer
Currently the various training institutions offer several programmes which include mechanical engineering, automotive engineering, tourism and hospitality studies, electrical power engineering, textile technology, agriculture, carpentry and joinery, solar installation and repairs, cosmetology, brick and block laying, fitting and turning plumbing among others. In addition, the VTCs offer cross cutting disciplines such as entrepreneurship and Information Communication Technology (ICT) studies. The Committee noted that there is need for modernization on the courses offered and improve towards technology and IT courses such as web designing. Training is being conducted both in-situ and Community Skills Outreach Programmes (CSOP) using the training for enterprise model whose hallmark is to inculcate a culture of entrepreneurship.
4.4 Certification
The Training for Enterprise (TFE) programme offers a range of certificates which include completion certificate, competence certificate, national foundation certificate and the TFE National Certificate. The courses span from weekly programmes to more comprehensive 6 months and 2 year programmes. The tiered structure allows learners to progress through the programmes at their own pace whilst achieving recognized certification at each level. The tiered structure allows students to go through examinations when they feel that they’re ready to be tested and not the orthodox approach of fixed examination period for all students.
4.5 Staffing issues
4.5.1 The department has an approved staff establishment of 795 posts but currently only 573 posts are filled, leaving a deficit of 213 vacant posts. The ideal staff establishment would be 1 600 skills trainers. It was highlighted that the Ministry was facing challenges in filling up the vacant posts. This had resulted in most of them relying heavily on part time trainers to fill the gap. The staff Detailed Establishment Table (DET) was only approving funding for the traditional old courses such as brick-laying and carpentry and not catering for new upcoming programs especially those on demand in the market.
4.5.2 The Committee found out that chief among the challenges was inadequate staffing which is prevalent at all VTCs. Both significantly big institutions such as Kaguvi VTC and small ones such as Mutare Urban VTC had this serial challenges on inadequate staffing. For instance the Committee noted that at Mutare Urban and Umguza VTC the staffing levels were at 60%. Umguza has 14 staff against a required umber of 24 teaching staff. This adversely affected training, administration as well as production at the institutions. VTCs such as Kaguvi had resorted to employing part time trainers and this has an impact on financial resources amassed through the production units. For instance, in Umguza, Matebeleland North District, the Committee noted that the acute shortage of trainers was a result of high number of vacancies that existed which need to be filled.
4.6 Zimbabwe Manpower Development Fund (ZIMDEF)
According to the Manpower Planning and Development Act (Chapter 28:02), Vocational Training institutions were there to provide any form of technical or vocational training whether personal or by correspondence, which is intended to qualify or is capable of qualifying such a person in whole or in part for any profession, calling or trade. Section 47 (2) of the act further highlights that ZIMDEF was established for the “development of skilled manpower in Zimbabwe”. To this end, VTCs contributed to the development of the much needed skilled manpower in the country and the Fund was solely earmarked for that purpose hence the facilities should have access to the fund which can go a long in the modernization and the retooling thrust in line with the country’s Vision. The same Fund has been instrumental in the setting up of innovation hubs at institutions of higher learning and therefore, can be extended to vocational training centres to become hubs of solutions and modern training.
4.7 Regional Best Practices
Vocational training is a key solution to youth unemployment and bridging the gap in skill shortages. Countries such as Ghana, Mauritius and Kenya have structured national frameworks, industry-relevant curricula, and government-supported reforms fueling vocational training growth and ultimately economic development. The Kenyan government established the Kenya National Qualifications Framework (KNQF) to streamline certification and progression and it prioritized public-private partnerships to modernize equipment, improve trainer capacity and introduce ICT into the curriculum. The aforementioned countries also promoted youth entrepreneurship through vocational training by offering start-up kits, incubation centres, and access to micro-finance. These models highlighted the importance of institutional coordination, industry linkages, and policy support in building responsive and sustainable vocational training systems.
4.8 Key challenges
The challenges obtained across the VTCs in the country are largely similar, however there are some that are unique and centre specific.
4.8.1 Staffing
The Committee noted that the shortage of teaching staff or trainers was a perennial challenge across all the institutions visited across the country. There was no institution with adequate staffing levels. Furthermore the Ministry was facing challenges in filling up the vacant posts.
4.8.2 Obsolete infrastructure, tools and equipment
Most of the equipment at the various centres as noted at Mutare Urban VTC was outdated and no longer commensurate with the modern technological trends and digital infrastructure that is being used in the industry and at other training institutions. The tools and equipment at some centres such as Masvingo Urban VTC were for home use and not for large-scale training as was the case at other centres visited.
4.8.3 Boarding facilities
The importance of adequate learning lecture-rooms and boarding facilities cannot be overemphasized. The Committee was disheartened to observe and receive submissions indicating that at Umguza VTC there was an all-inclusive convertible single room which served as a classroom during the day and turned into a hostel at night. This was not a conducive set-up for learning. The staff housing was also a major concern at most of the centres. Many VTCs are housed in dilapidated buildings with insufficient classrooms except for major centres such as Mushagashe, Magamba and Kaguvi VTCs. Some of the boarding facilities were in a dilapidated state as was the case at Phangani VTC. 
4.8.4 Non or late release of funds
The late release of funds by Treasury hampered training activities and the implementation of viable production units. The payment of part-time trainers from retained funds compromises the procurement of training materials and other activities that are of paramount importance to the effective operation of VTCs. This has been exacerbated by the non-release of funds by Treasury. In 2024 alone, funds disbursed for operations were equivalent to US$1 628 per VTC per term. Given that there are 60 VTCs in the country, the amount disbursed for operations was small and could not produce the expected impact, with the majority of VTCS failing to access Treasury funding. It is also important to note that the funds that were released by Treasury had a bearing on the total fees that is to be paid by the students. The release of funds by Treasury tends to subsidize what is eventually paid by students thereby making the education accessible and affordable.
4.8.5 Transport/ Immobility
Most centres have no service vehicles whilst VTC programming requires a lot of mobility within and outside respective districts and provinces.
4.8.6 Land limitation
Adequate space for the needs of a modern VTC operation was of paramount importance. Mutare Urban and Masvingo Urban VTCs do not have conducive workshop space for training, gardening and even sporting facilities. Mutare Urban VTC is situated at an old beerhall and nothing much can be done to have adequate space for a conducive learning and training environment that involved practical sessions. Furthermore it was highlighted that some centres that were at rented facilities from town councils were restricted from renovations because of the lease agreements in operation and were operating in a confined place with no further renovations that could be carried out.
5.0 Committee Observations
5.1 The Committee observed that there is need for the modernization of the curriculum to ensure that courses currently offered at VTC institutions incorporated new modern courses such as cellphone repairs, web designing and development, graphic design, coding and climate smart gardening among others, which were in demand. The courses that were being offered are the traditional ones and hence may not attract and capture the whole youth demographic. It was noted that Information Communication Technology (ICT) was being offered as a subject to all students but did not offer certificates.
5.2 The Committee observed that the certificates issued by vocational training institutions in various trades were not recognized as equivalent qualifications when one wants to advance their studies. At the completion of each course the trainee was presented with a certificate of completion/ competence with a particular requisite skill. However, when one decided to further their studies at other institutions of higher education without having attained the required number of Ordinary level subjects, the acquired skill from the VTCs was not recognized to supplement their eligibility criteria in a similar discipline of study. Currently only the motor mechanics course was recognized as a substitute to a qualification and this has to be widened to include other courses offered.
5.3 The impact of the CSOP was well commended and widening the net for those accessing vocational education and essential skills. This programme had to be supported as it was assisting many youths. In addition the building brigades that were at the various institutions were actually producing services and products that were responding and solving societal problems.
5.4 It was further noted that the fees structure, at the institutions on average was USD $ 500.00 per term, which seemed to be too much beyond the reach of many prospective students. The fees are actually higher than what is being charged by other universities and this in-turn tends to shun away many youth who may have possibly dropped out of school due to unavailability of tuition fees. The obtaining high fees further exacerbates the situation and makes vocational training become a reserve for only a few who can afford it. A notable step has been the introduction of the “work for fees programmes” which has assisted quite a number of students to enroll for training. This is a commendable move towards the realization of the country’s vision and Ministry’s thrust. This may have to be spread to other training institutions for it to cater for more students.
5.5 Most institutions were in dire need of funding to revamp the facilities that ought to be providing education, training and skills development. It was observed by the Committee that urban and rural training centres had different peculiar needs hence there was need for a responsive model of funding that caters for the different centres. For instance urban centres needed land to expand their operations whereas most rural institutions had the land but lacked the financial capacity to adequately equip the facilities and enhance mobility. The training centres used a blanket approach in offering courses. There was need to introduce area specific courses in-order to cater for the needs and skills gap in the respective areas. Some training centres were sitting on vast tracts of land which was being under-utilised. It was noted that in mining areas such as Phangani, Kadoma and Zvishavane there were no mining courses being offered yet the areas were powerhouses in such trades.
5.6 The Committee further observed the importance of timeous disbursement of budgeted funds to VTCs as it aided in improving the quality of the education being offered and contributes to the development of the youth demography. It was further noted that the disbursements made towards the operations of VTCs in the last year were a drop in the ocean as it was not adequate to cater for the bear minimum requirements for their operations. Timeous disbursement of funds could also improve the general operations of the institutions and the quality of education offered.
5.7 Some of the VTCs were situated in areas where there is poor network. Internet connectivity is of paramount importance in the advancement of any educational prospects and there was need to provide mobile network infrastructure across the country. Centres were also affected by erratic electricity supply which worsened the electricity woes. Phangani VTC has an overhead electricity debt which has seen the institution operating in darkness for almost a year.
5.8 Production units need capitalization in order to supplement the limited resources available as they allow VTCs to generate some income and assist in the smooth operations of the institutions. There were competitively produced products from the VTCs which could impact the market space and have the potential to be run commercially and generate significant revenue for the institutions. There was need for the registration of the production units to allow them to compete in the tendering process.
5.9 The shortage of staff was prevalent across all the institutions with limited critical trainers for various courses on offer. This adversely affects training, administration as well as production. Some of the VTCs have resorted to employing part time trainers and this had a huge impact on financial resources. The situation was worsened by the fact that the Public Service Commission (PSC) did not provide staff for some of the courses on their Detailed Staff Establishment Table (DET) which shows the approved jobs and number of posts created for the normal and regular requirements of the institution.
6.0 Recommendations
The Ministry of Youth Empowerment, Development and Vocational Training should revise the current curriculum in-order to incorporate and introduce digital literacy and emerging technological modern courses such as web designing, IT-tech AI as stand-alone courses in line with industry demands by 31 December, 2025. Additionally the Ministry should introduce area specific programmes depending with the area.
The Ministry of Higher and Tertiary Education, Innovation, Science and Technology Development should recognize as entry requirements substitutes for courses and standardize the qualifications obtained through VTCs by 31 March, 2026.
The Ministry of Finance and Economic Development should timeously disburse allocated funds to the Ministry of Youth Empowerment, Development and Vocational Training towards the maintenance, operations and retooling of VTCs by 31 September, 2025.
The Ministry of Youth Empowerment, Development and Vocational Training should by 31 August, 2025 review the fees structure at VTCs downwards to about USD $250.00 per term, this should be coupled with timeous disbursements of allocated fund by the Ministry of Finance, Economic Development and Investment Promotion to supplement the fees paid. In addition the Ministry should come up with an equitable fees structure based on the status of the centre and its location to ensure that no one is left behind in accessing vocational education by 31 October, 2025.
The Ministry of Public Service, Labour and Social Welfare should fill in the vacancies at VTCs by 31 October, 2025 and have a detailed establishment table that includes modern courses to be offered.
The Ministry of Information Communication Technology (ICT), Postal and Courier Services should facilitate the repurposing of post offices into centres of data excellence for vocational training and facilitate internet connectivity at training institutions that are currently out of reach by, 31 December, 2025.
The Ministry of Youth Empowerment, Development and Vocational Training should collaborate with industries to facilitate apprenticeships, internships, and real-world skill application and build synergies with other sister training institutions such as the School of Mines and other universities by 31 March 2026.
The Ministry of Youth Empowerment, Development and Vocational Training should facilitate the registration and commercialization of VTCs production units to allow them to compete for tenders by 31 December, 2025.
The Ministry of Higher and Tertiary Education, Innovation, Science and Technology Development and Treasury should allow access to part of the ZIMDEF funding to VTCs, by 31 September, 2025.
The Ministry of Youth Empowerment, Development and Vocational Training should benchmark with other centres in the region to have best practices in the vocational training sector by 31 March, 2026.
The Ministry of Lands, Agriculture, Water, Fisheries and Rural Development; the Ministry Local Government and Public Works; and the Ministry of National Housing should facilitate the provision of land and space for vocational training within local authorities and rural areas and also ensure that emerging housing schemes have adequate space for VTCs by 31 December, 2025.
7.0 Conclusion
VTCs empower the youth with entrepreneurial skills to venture into business and significantly contribute to the country’s domestic product (GDP). Similarly, excellent VTC facilities and infrastructure attracts better performance resulting in abundant socio-economic benefits such as employment creation and revenue generation, providing and equipping the youth with relevant life skills, fighting the cancerous scourge of drug and substance abuse, among others. Thus, there is an urgent need for a coordinated approach to the revitalization of the country’s VTCs.
In line with the country’s Vision of attaining an Upper Middle Class Income Society by 2030 means that the VTCs infrastructure need to be modernized to a level that is in tandem with the vision. Modernizing vocational training centers is critical to building a skilled workforce and fostering economic growth. The Committee urges the Government and relevant stakeholders to implement the recommended measures to ensure that vocational education remains relevant, competitive and accessible to all.
