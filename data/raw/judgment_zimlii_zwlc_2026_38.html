GONESI J:

  1. THE APPEAL

This is an appeal against the decision of an Arbitrator filed in terms of Rule 19(1) of the Labour Court Rules, 2017. The appellant’s grounds of appeal are as follows:

  1. The honourable arbitrator grossly erred at law in making the irrational finding that the 2nd appellant received a retrenchment package from the 1st respondent and retained it, and therefore waived her rights, when it was common cause that she did not receive a retrenchment package, or any other form of package, from the 1st respondent.



  1. Further, the honourable arbitrator grossly erred and seriously misdirected himself on a question of law in finding that the appellants' alleged delay in returning funds which were deposited into the bank accounts of some of them without their knowledge, let alone consent, meant, on its own, that they had waived their rights to challenge the unlawful termination of their employment.





  1. Furthermore, the honourable arbitrator erred in finding that the purported retrenchments were lawful and procedural when it was common cause that the first attempt to retrench the appellants was aborted and the second attempt to retrench them was contrary to the law as no notice of retrenchment was given and no packages were paid on the date of termination of employment.



  1. The honourable arbitrator also grossly misdirected himself in making the irrational finding that the purported retrenchments which were a direct result of the 1st respondent's numerous losses in court at the hands of the appellants were not motivated by a desire to punish and harass the appellants.





  1. The honourable arbitrator grossly misdirected himself and erred at law in not finding that as there was no valid reason for laying off the appellants, the immediate recruitment of persons to fill the appellants' positions being clear proof of the 1" respondent's need for their services and capacity to pay them, the purported retrenchments were unlawful and therefore invalid.



  1. RELIEF SOUGHT



Wherefore appellant prays for the following relief:



a) The appeal be and is hereby allowed with costs.



b) The arbitration award of honourable arbitrator L. M. Gabilo dated 8 July 2025 be and is hereby set aside and replaced with the

i. The termination of the appellants employment by the 1st respondent in 2018 be and is hereby set aside as it was unlawful.



ii. The 1st respondent is directed to reinstate the appellants without loss of salaries or benefits, or pay them damages in lieu of reinstatement, if reinstatement is no longer tenable, the quantum of which shall be determined by the Labour Court upon application.



  1. FACTUAL BACKGROUND

The appellants are former employees of the 1st respondent. The 1st appellant was employed as an Officer Orderly on a one-year contract. The 2nd appellant was employed in June 2003 as a Terminal Services Assistant on a contract without limit of time. The 3rd appellant was employed as a Risk Officer on 1 March 2002 as an Internal Auditor. The 4th appellant was employed on 1 September 2008 as an Aviation Security Officer Grade 3.2. The 5th appellant was employed on 8 November 1999 as a Cashier.

In 2017, the 1st respondent notified the 1st to the 5th respondents of its intention to retrench them. On 15 March 2018, the respondent invited the appellants to attend a meeting to negotiate a retrenchment package. On the 22nd May 2018, before any retrenchment package had been agreed upon, the claimants lodged a complaint with the National Employment Council for the Air Transport Industry. In 2025 the parties appeared before an arbitrator whose terms of reference were:

  1. Whether or not the Labour Officer has jurisdiction over the matter.

  2. Whether or not the employees conduct constituted a waiver.

  3. Whether or not the matter is prescribed.

  4. Whether or not the retrenchment was lawful

  5. The remedy thereof.

It was the Arbitrator’s finding that the Labour Officer had jurisdiction to preside over the matter and subsequently refer the matter to compulsory arbitration in terms of section 93(5)(c) of the Labour Act. It was also the Arbitrator’s finding that the appellants’ failure to return the retrenchment packages for a period of five years when they were paid to them constituted a waiver. The Arbitrator also found that the matter had not prescribed. The Arbitrator went on to make a finding that the retrenchment was lawfully carried out despite the appellants raising procedural irregularities on the process used by the 1st respondent. The reasons for such a finding by the Arbitrator were that the 1st respondent notified appellants in writing of his intention to retrench them in a letter dated 20 December 2017, authored by T. Gusha, Acting General Manager at the time, as required by the retrenchment regulations. The letters, according to the arbitrator, indicated different packages depending on the positions and length of service of each claimant, based on minimum retrenchment packages.

The Arbitrator stated that in terms of Section 12c (1) of the Labour Amendment Act No 15 of 2015 employer is required to give notice of his intention (1) to the works council of the establishment or undertaking or (ii) if there is no works council established of the undertaking or to the employment council or to the retrenchment board provide with details of every employee whom the employer wishes to retrench and the reasons for the proposed retrenchment. The Arbitrator stated that the 1st respondent notified the works council of its intention to retrench through a letter dated 12th of October 2017 which contained names of the employees to be retrenched and the reasons thereof. It was stated that a reading of the submissions filed of record there is evidence that all appellants were given letters of retrenchment dated 20 December 2017 with details of minimum packages to be paid to each appellant in line with the Labour Act. The arbitrator went on to award as follows,

“In view of the foregoing consideration and legal provisions examined, I hereby conclude that in all the retrenchment that was conducted by respondent was lawful and in line with section 12c (1) of the labour Act Amendment no 15 of 2015. Accordingly, I order that:

1. Respondent pay retrenchment package to claimants as indicated in their letters of 20 December 2017 previously offered and accepted.

2. Respondent pay terminal benefits to claimants in line with section 13 of the Labour Act.

3. Respondent to pay pensions to claimants in terms of Industry pensions fund rules

4. Each party to meet its legal costs.”



  1. PROCEEDINGS BEFORE THIS COURT



APPELLANT’S CASE

4.1. In motivating the appeal, Mr Gama submitted that it was a settled principle of law that a retrenchment process that does not comply with the provisions of the act was null and void. In making these submissions, counsel referred the court to the following authorities :Kadir & Sons Pvt Ltd vs Panganayi & Anor 1996(1) ZLR 598 (SC)

Prosser & Ors vs Zimbabwe Iron & Steel Company HH 201/93

Stanbic Bank of Zimbabwe Ltd -vs -Charamba 2006 1 ZLR 96 SC

Chidziva & Ors vs Zimbabwe Iron & Steel Co. Ltd 1997 2 ZLR 368.

Mr Gama argued further that the authorities stated clearly that the failure to comply with the mandatory requirements of the retrenchment process invalidated the retrenchment.



4.2. It was the contention by Mr Gama that the respondent in casu failed to comply with the provisions of the then section 12(c) of the Labour act which provided the procedure to be followed on retrenchment. Counsel argued further that in order to comply with the retrenchment process , the 1st respondent was obliged to pay the appellants no later than the 31st December 2017.



4.3. Mr Gama argued that the second retrenchment process was procedurally irregular in that the 1st Respondent failed to follow the procedure stipulated in the Labour Act. Counsel submitted further that the retrenchment process was unfair if one considers that the appellants had occupied these positions for more than 5 years. How does a person become excess to the manpower development of an organisation after holding the office for 10 years , so argued Counsel. The retrenchment was retaliation and harassment of an employee who had approached a court of law for redress against an employer, Mr Gama argued.



4.4. In addressing the issue of waiver, as a way of introduction, Mr Gama submitted that the facts of the case at hand were clearly distinguishable from the Chidziva case. Counsel argued further that in the Chidziva case, the employees had accepted the retrenchment packages and later on sought to challenge the retrenchment process, which is not the position in casu as the appellants did not accept any package.



4.5. In actual fact , the appellants were asked to sign some forms before the retrenchment package could be paid and such forms were never signed by the appellants, so submitted Counsel. It was the submission by Mr Gama that before the retrenchment package had been paid, the appellants challenged the said retrenchment when they filed a complaint through the National Employment Council for the Air Industry. Furthermore, Counsel argued that had the appellants accepted the retrenchment package, they would have filed a withdrawal of the complaint against employer. This showed that appellants never abandoned their right to challenge the retrenchment process.



4.6. Mr Gama submitted that the 2nd appellant did not receive any payment from the 1st respondent to date. Counsel submitted further that the 1st respondent deposited some funds into the 1st, 3rd, 4th and 5th appellant’s bank accounts. When the appellants became aware that funds had been transferred, they continued to prosecute their case against the 1st respondent and in addition to that they returned the funds to the 1st respondent , so argued Counsel. It was the contention by Counsel that a delay in acting did not constitute waiver.



1ST RESPONDENT’S CASE.

4.7 Per contra, Mr Duve submitted that the findings by the Honourable Gabilo were correct at law. It was misplaced for the appellants to convey a story of victimisation and allege that there were two attempts to retrench the appellants, which was incorrect, argued Counsel. Counsel submitted that the retrenchment procedure was adhered to and hence the process was above board.



4.8 Mr Duve argued that by merely accepting the package, appellants waived their right to challenge the retrenchment process. Counsel relied on the authority in the Chidziva case. It was the contention by counsel that an appreciation of the Chidziva case would show that it was exactly what the appellants in casu were trying to do, yet the Supreme Court decisively dealt with the issue that upon acceptance of the package, one would have waived the right to challenge the retrenchment process.



4.9. Counsel argued further that for the appellants to allege that it took them 5 years to realise that they had money in their accounts was highly misleading. The packages were a huge sum of money such that no one could fail to recognise that there has been a deposit of funds, so argued Counsel.



4.10 Mr Duve argued that considering the doctrine of peremption, the conduct by the appellants of receiving the retrenchment package in 2018 and returning in 2023, they could not allege that they were not in agreement with the retrenchment process. Counsel relied on the principle enunciated in the case of Hlatshwayo vs Mare Deas 1912 AD pg 242. Mr Duve submitted that it was clear that the appellants in receiving the money, they had waived their right . By merely accepting, they insisted on the strict right of waiver against challenging the retrenchment proceedings so argued Counsel. They could not insist on two conflicting positions so submitted Mr Duve.



4.11. Mr Duve contended further that the 1st respondent had been prejudiced financially by the conduct of the appellants in receiving the money in USD dollars and tendering the amount in local currency.



4.12. Counsel submitted with regards to the 2nd appellant that the money meant for the retrenchment bounced back and pleaded with the court to make a finding that she had also received a retrenchment package during the same time that the appellants had the money deposited into their accounts.



  1. ISSUES FOR DETERMINATION

Even though in the notice of appeal the appellants raised 5 grounds of appeal, the grounds of appeal raise the following cardinal issue for determination:

  1. Whether or not the arbitrator erred in law and fact in upholding the validity of the appellants’ retrenchment. In other words, was the retrenchment of the appellants in terms of the law.



To properly make an assessment it is important to establish facts that are common cause.

COMMON CAUSE.

    1. On the 17th October 2017, a letter titled Notice of intention to retrench was addressed to the appellants by the 1st respondent.



    1. On the 18th December 2017, the 1st respondent wrote again to the appellants advising further that the appellants must stop reporting for duty beginning the 19th December 2017.



    1. On the 20th December 2017, a letter was addressed to the appellants by the 1st respondent advising them that the effective date of retrenchment was the 31st December 2017.





    1. On the 15th March 2018, the appellants were invited to attend a meeting whose agenda was to discuss their retrenchment package. The meeting was to be held on the 22nd March 2018.



    1. A letter was addressed to 1st respondent by appellants’ legal practitioners whose contents were inter alia that the appellants would not be attending the meeting to discuss a package as it was their belief that since the purported retrenchment was aborted, the appellants were entitled to report for duty.





    1. On the 22nd May 2018, the appellants filed a complaint with the National Employment Council for the Air Industry against the 1st respondent.



    1. Letters for retrenchment were written again to the appellants on the 23rd May 2018. In the letter, the 1st respondent confirmed the date of retrenchment to be the 31st March 2018.





    1. The 1st ,3rd, 4th and 5th appellants had money deposited into their accounts in 2018, which money was tendered back to the 1st respondent in 2023.



    1. The 2nd respondent never received any payment in the form of a retrenchment package from the 1st respondent.





Having established the common cause factors, the following questions exercised the mind of the court:

  • Was there a retrenchment as envisaged by section 12 (c ) of the Labour Act ?

  • Did the deposit of the “retrenchment package” by the 1st respondent without consent and agreement of the appellant amount to acceptance of retrenchment?

  • Did the return of the “retrenchment package “ after 5 years mean a waiver of the right to challenge the retrenchment process ?

  • Did the appellants accept and retain the retrenchment package?

  • Did their conduct show that they acquiesced to the process and package paid by the respondent.

  • Was the arbitrator correct in making a finding that the retrenchment process was lawful?



  1. ANALYSIS OF EVIDENCE



6.1. At the commencement of the proceedings, the court had advised the parties to try and pursue an out of court settlement to enable the parties to come up with a lasting resolution to the dispute between them. It is rather unfortunate that parties reported back that they could not find each other and the court must proceed to make a determination on the matter.



6.2. It is trite to note that the borne of contention in this matter is the validity of the retrenchment which was done by the 1st respondent. Our law is very clear on the process to be undertaken on retrenchment. It provides thus:



12C Retrenchment and compensation for loss of employment on retrenchment or in terms of section 12(4a) (1) An employer who wishes to retrench any one or more employees shall— (a) give written notice of his or her intention—

  1. (ii) (iii) and to the works council established for the undertaking;

  2. or if there is no works council established for the undertaking or if a majority of the employees concerned agree to such a course, to the employment council established for the undertaking or industry;

  3. or if there is no works council or employment council for the undertaking concerned, to the Retrenchment Board, and in such event any reference in this section to the performance of functions by a works council or employment council shall be construed as a reference to the Retrenchment Board or a person appointed by the Board to perform such functions on its behalf;

  4. (b) provide the works council, employment council or the Retrenchment Board, as the case may be, with details of every employee whom the employer wishes to retrench and of the reasons for the proposed retrenchment;

  5. and (c) send a copy of the notice to the Retrenchment Board.

  6. (2) Unless better terms are agreed between the employer and employees concerned or their representatives, a package (hereinafter called “the minimum retrenchment package”) of not less than one month’s salary or wages for every two years of service as an employee (or the equivalent lesser proportion of one month’s salary or wages for a lesser period of service) shall be paid by the employer as compensation for loss of employment (whether the loss of employment is occasioned by retrenchment or by virtue of termination of employment pursuant to section 12(4a)(a), (b) or (c)),no later than date when the notice of termination of employment takes effect. (3) Where an employer alleges financial incapacity and consequent inability to pay the minimum retrenchment package timeously or at all, the employer shall apply in writing to be exempted from paying the full minimum retrenchment package or any part of it to— (a) the employment council established for the undertaking or industry; or (b) if there is no employment council for the undertaking concerned, to the Retrenchment Board; which shall respond to the request within fourteen days of receiving the notice (failing which response the application is deemed to have been granted). (4) In considering its response to a request for exemption in terms of subsection (3) the employment council or Retrenchment Board— (a) shall, where the employer alleges complete inability to pay the minimum retrenchment package, be entitled to demand and receive such proof as it considers requisite to satisfy itself that the employer is so unable, and if so unable on the date when the notice of termination of employment takes effect, may propose to the employer a scheme to pay the minimum retrenchment package by instalments over a period of time; (b) shall, where the employer offers to pay the minimum retrenchment package by instalments over a period of time, consider whether the offer is a reasonable one, and may propose an alternative payment schedule; (c) may inquire from the employer whether he or she has considered, or may wish to consider, specifically or in general, the alternatives to termination of employment provided for in section 12D



6.4. In this case, it is abundantly clear that the process of retrenchment was not followed. The purported retrenchment package was not deposited on the effective date of retrenchment envisaged by the 1st respondent. The act provides that the retrenchment package shall be deposited “….no later than date when the notice of termination of employment takes effect “. In casu , this was not done. It was incumbent on the 1st respondent to provide the proof that indeed due process was followed in conducting the retrenchment. It is the finding of the court that the purported retrenchment was marred with irregularities and as such the Honourable arbitrator fell into error in making a conclusion that the retrenchment process was lawful and such a finding ought to be vacated by allowing the present appeal. Bartlett J in the Posser case at page 3 of the cyclostyled judgement observed that “…The regulations lay down a procedure to be followed and it is important that the procedure be followed. I say that especially in regard to retrenchment matters because retrenchment by any standard is a traumatic procedure. This is particularly so for persons who have been in employment for many years,-16 years in Prossers’ case. It is important that against such an emotionally traumatic climate, that employers should simply and clearly follow the law.”

6.5. Having established that the retrenchment process was irregular, was it competent for the appellants to challenge the said process after a “retrenchment package” had been deposited into the bank accounts?

In answering the above question, guidance will be sought from the retrenchment letter addressed to the appellants dated 23 May 2018.

“SUBJECT:LETTER OF RETRENCHMENT

The above matter refers.

Reference is made to our letter dated 20 December 2017. Please be advised that:

  1. Following failure to reach an agreement with you regarding a retrenchment package the Authority is left with no option but to pay you the minimum retrenchment package as guided by the law, section 12C of the Labour Act, Amendment No. 5 of 2015.

  2. The effective date of termination which will be used to calculate the retrenchment package is 31 March 2018.

  3. The notice period was 1 January to 31 March 2018.

  4. Note that the April 2018 salary payment was done in error and will be recovered from your terminal benefits.

We have enclosed the following forms for your action:

  • Clearance form on Termination STF 039

  • Pension Termination Form

Your terminal benefits including pension withdrawal will be released to you upon receipt of the duly completed forms.

Yours Faithfully”

6.6. A look at the letter will bring to the fore that funds were deposited into appellant’s accounts without completion of the relevant paperwork. Can this unilateral deposit of funds be taken as an acceptance of the retrenchment and effectively a bar to challenge the process? The answer is a no. I say so because the appellants continued challenging the retrenchment process , and not only that, they tendered back the money they had received from the 1st respondent, albeit after 5 years. The question is did the appellants fill in the forms, secondly, did they know that money had been deposited into their accounts and the answer is still in the negative.

6.7. It is trite law that there is strong presumption against waiver and the onus is on the party asserting waiver to prove it. In Hepner Roodepoort Maraisburg Town Council 1962 (4) SA 772 A where Steyn CJ said at 778 D-F “There is authority for the view that in the case of waiver by conduct, the conduct must leave no reasonable doubt as to the intention of surrendering the right in issue. In the present case, no real attempt was made to show that the appellants abandoned their rights with full knowledge of those rights. All that was submitted was that the appellants accepted the retrenchment packages. The respondent should have gone further to show that they did this with full knowledge that they were abandoning their rights. On this I also cite with approval the passages at pg 489 of Christie’s book where the learned author said “…..there is ample other authority that it must be clearly proved that the person who is alleged to have waived his rights knew what those rights were…” Delay in enforcing a contractual right is not necessarily a waiver of the right. One can go further and say the delay of itself and without more, can never deprive a party to a contractual right except by prescription.” The observations in above apply with equal force to the case at hand.

6.8. In Chidziva & ors -vs- Zimbabwe Steel Co. Ltd 1997 (2) ZLR 368 SC when quoting Mutual Life Ins Co of New York vs Ingle 1910 TPD the court held that “….when a person entitled to a right knows that it is being infringed, and by his acquiescence leads the person infringing it to think that he has abandoned it, then he would under certain circumstances be debarred from asserting it.”

Dumbutshena CJ in Barclays Bank of Zimbabwe Ltd -vs- Binga Products Pvt Ltd 1985 (3) SA said “ I seek, however to highlight the principle of waiver set out by Lord Denning Mr at 140a-c where he said “The principle of waiver is simply this, if one party by his conduct, leads another to believe that the strict rights arising under the contract will not be insisted on, intending that the other should act on that belief, and he does act on it. Then the first party will not afterwards be allowed to insist on the strict legal rights when it would be inequitable for him to do so… “ This is not what happened in the case before the court, the appellants openly challenged the retrenchment process in more than one way. Apart from returning the deposited funds, they continued in litigation against the 1st respondent, can such conduct qualify as a waiver?

6.9. The Chidziva case is distinguishable from the case at hand in that in casu the appellants did not accept the retrenchment package, the 1st respondent unilaterally deposited the funds into the accounts of the 1st, 3rd , 4th and 5th appellants and the appellants tendered it back.

6.10. The letters written to the appellants on 20 December 2017 informing them of their retrenchment do not suffice as notice of intention to retrench. In the same letter, it is stated that the effective date of retrenchment is 31 December 2017. The law is also very clear that the retrenchment package should be paid no later than the date when the notice of termination of employment takes effect. It should be noted in this case that this was not followed. The 1st respondent deposited funds in the accounts of the appellants without their knowledge.

6.11.Mr Duve sought to argue that through the doctrine of peremption, the appellants could not challenge the retrenchment process as their conduct exhibited acquiescence. This argument does not find favour with the court considering that the position of the appellants has always been clear, that is, they were challenging the retrenchment process.

6.12. Furthermore, the 1st respondent cannot claim financial prejudice especially if a consideration is made that the 1st respondent in a way forced payment on the appellants by taking advantage of the bank details that were at their disposal as the employer.

It is therefore clear that the finding by the arbitrator is contrary to law and such constituted a misdirection entailing the findings to be set aside.

DISPOSITION

  1. The appeal be and is hereby allowed with costs.

  2. The arbitration award by the honourable arbitrator L.M Gabilo dated 8 July 2025 be and is hereby set aside and substituted with the following order:

"The termination of the appellants' employment by the 1st respondent be and is hereby set aside as it was unlawful"

  1. The 1st respondent be and is hereby directed to reinstate the appellants without any loss of salary and benefits, or pay them damages in lieu of reinstatement, if reinstatement is no longer tenable.

  2. The parties shall agree on the quantum of damages, failing which either party may approach the court for quantification.
