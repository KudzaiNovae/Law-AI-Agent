CHIVIZHE, J:


This is an appeal against the determination by the Appeals Committee dated 23 January

2025.

BACKGROUND FACTS

The Appellant was employed permanently by the Respondent as a Billing Clerk in Kadoma until his dismissal following a disciplinary hearing in which he was found guilty of disorderly or objectionable behaviour as provided for under section 7(1)e of the NEC for the Zimbabwe Energy Industry Code of Conduct and Grievance Handling Procedures,2011. Aggrieved by

the Disciplinary Committee’s determination, he lodged an appeal with the Appeals Committee, which appeal was dismissed on 21 January 2025. Being out of time to appeal to this court, the Appellant applied for condonation for late noting of appeal, which application was granted on 29 September 2025. The Appellant then filed this appeal on 6 October 2025.

GROUNDS OF APPEAL

  1. The Appeals Committee erred and misdirected itself in failing to find that it was improper for the disciplinary committee to rely on witnesses’ statements in the absence of viva voce evidence from the authors of the witnesses’ statements.

  2. The Appeals Committee grossly erred in finding the Appellant guilty of the acts of misconduct in the absence of proper and corroborated evidence adduced on behalf of the Respondent that the Appellant committed the act of misconduct.

  1. The Appeals Committee misdirected itself and grossly erred in failing to find that the Appellant managed to rebut all the unsubstantiated and unverified allegations against him.

The Appellant prays for the following relief:


“That the appeal succeeds with costs.


That the decision of the Appeals Committee be set aside and substituted as follows:


    1. The appeal to the Appeals Committee is upheld.


    1. The determination of the Disciplinary Committee is set aside and substituted as follows:


  1. The Appellant is found not guilty of the misconduct alleged.


  1. The Appellant is reinstated to his former position without loss of salary and benefits with effect from the date of dismissal.

  2. Should reinstatement not be possible, the parties must agree on damages in lieu of reinstatement within thirty (30) days of this determination, failing which either party may approach the Labour Court for purposes of quantification.”



PARTIES SUBMISSIONS


On the preliminary issue that the notice of appeal is fatally defective


The Respondent contended that the Notice of Appeal was fatally defective because it failed to specify the dies induciae within which it could file a response, thereby infringing on its procedural rights under the Labour Court Rules, 2017. The Respondent relied on authorities, including Jensen v Acavalos 1993 (1) ZLR 216 (S) and Zimbabwe Open University v Mazombwe 2009 (1) ZLR 101 (H) to submit that non-compliance with procedural requirements renders an appeal invalid and that condonation cannot be granted absent a plausible explanation for the failure to comply.

The Appellant submitted that the preliminary point raised by the Respondent that the Notice of Appeal was fatally defective lacked merit. He argued that the notice substantially complied with the Labour Court Rules, 2017, despite not specifying the dies induciae for filing a response. The Appellant also noted that the Respondent had filed its Notice of Response within the ten working days prescribed by Rule 29(2), thereby suffering no prejudice. The failure therefore did not render the application fatally defective. Reference was made to

Standard Chartered Bank vs Chikomwe 2004 (11) ZLR 1976: Zimplats vs Marko Phuti SC 21/16. Counsel emphasized that procedural rules are intended to facilitate justice rather than obstruct it and that minor procedural errors could be rectified by the court under Rule 32. Finally he submitted that labour disputes should generally not be disposed of on the basis of on technicalities the court should strive to resolve disputes on the merits. The prayer was for the point in limine to be dismissed for lack of merit.



EVALUATION OF PRELIMINARY POINT


Whether or not the notice of appeal is fatally defective


Having considered the parties submissions and the authorities referred to by them, the court finds that although the Notice of Appeal did not specify the dies induciae, this procedural defect, not having resulted in prejudice, is not fatal. The Respondent in this case was aware of the appeal, it filed its response in accordance with the Rules and appeared for the oral hearing. In such circumstances, the omission becomes technical rather than substantive, the omission is not fatal to the appeal.

The Court further notes that the Labour Court Rules 2017 permit the court to, in the exercise of its discretion, condone minor procedural defects in the interest of justice. In the exercise of such discretion as provided in Rule 32, the court hereby extends condonation to the Appellant for the failure to comply with the rules of this court. The preliminary objection is accordingly dismissed. The court shall proceed to consider the appeal on the merits.

SUBMISSIONS ON THE MERITS OF THE CASE


Regarding the substantive grounds of appeal, the Appellant main contention is that the Appels Committee erred and misdirected itself in failing to find that it was improper for the Disciplinary Committee to rely on the witness statements only in the absence of viva voce evidence from the authors of those statements. Under his second ground of appeal the Appellant contends that the Appeals Committee further erred and misdirected itself in confirming his conviction on the charge in the absence of proper and corroborative evidence having being placed before the Disciplinary Committee. In regards the last ground of appeal the Appellant contends that the Appeals Committee grossly erred in failing to give regard to the fact that he had during the Disciplinary Committee hearing managed to rebut all the allegations as made against him. The Appellant, has by specific reference to the record of proceedings before the

Disciplinary Committee highlighted the deficiencies in the evidence as tendered for the employer. He submits that he did in the hearing refute allegations linking him to four alleged transactions involving the sale of tokens, on the basis that in one transaction no witnesses were called to confirm the transaction, in another transaction the client involved had not been identified, in yet another transaction no evidence of the financial loss suffered by the Respondent was led. The Appellant underscored that the evidence for the employer was led through the Complaint who was unable to substantiate on the deficiencies in the evidence tendered. It is Appellant contention that in view of the insufficient and unreliable evidence led the Respondent failed to discharge the burden on it to prove the charges.

The Appellant further argues that the Appeals Committee improperly upheld the finding that he failed to rebut allegations or demonstrate innocence in association with a co- accused, despite the Respondent having failed in the disciplinary hearing to establish any connection between him and the said co-accused.

The Appellant maintained that the Appeals Committee erred in upholding the Disciplinary Committee’s decision, as the Respondent failed to provide credible or corroborated evidence linking him to any misconduct. The Appellant therefore submitted that the appeal is meritorious and should succeed.

Per contra, the Respondent argued that the Appellant’s claim that the disciplinary committee improperly relied on witness statements without viva voce evidence was legally unsound. The Respondent relied on the case of Chataira v ZESA SC 83/01 and maintained that internal disciplinary proceedings do not require oral testimony or cross-examination, provided the employee is made aware of the allegations, given an opportunity to respond, and the tribunal acts in good faith. The Respondent submitted that the Appellant was afforded all procedural safeguards, had access to the statements, and was able to make representations before the committee.

The Respondent further argued that the evidence before the Disciplinary Committee comprising detailed witness statements and transaction records was sufficient, credible, and properly linked the Appellant to the alleged misconduct. In contrast, the Appellant’s defence outline to the allegations consisted largely of bare denials and lacked substantive rebuttal. The Respondent emphasized that the standard of proof in disciplinary proceedings, being civil proceedings, remains the same that it is on a balance of probabilities, which standard was correctly applied by both the Disciplinary and Appeals Committees. The Respondent also noted

that appellate courts are slow to interfere with factual findings unless they are grossly unreasonable, a standard clearly not met in this case.

In respect of all grounds of appeal, the Respondent maintained that the Appeals Committee acted within procedural and substantive fairness, properly evaluated the evidence, and reached a legally sound decision. Accordingly, the Respondent prayed that the appeal be dismissed in its entirety with costs

ISSUE FOR DETERMINATION


Whether or not the grounds of appeal are vested with merits.


THE LAW


In Chataira v ZESA SC 83/01, the Supreme Court held that in disciplinary proceedings:


  1. An employee must be made aware of the accusations against them.


  1. They must be afforded an opportunity to present their case.


  1. The disciplinary authority must act in good faith.


  1. Viva voce evidence is not mandatory; the employee need only be shown the statements or documentary evidence relied upon.

Similarly, in Jerry Musarira v Anglo American Corporation SC 53/05, the Court emphasized the core principles of natural justice (audi alteram partem) as:

  1. Adequate notice of the hearing must be given.


  1. The employee must have an opportunity to be heard and to present their case.


  1. The employee should be allowed to call witnesses if they wish.


These provisions ensure that disciplinary proceedings comply with the principles of natural justice.

APPLICATION OF THE LAW TO THE FACTS


The central issue in this appeal is whether the Appeals Committee erred in upholding the Disciplinary Committee’s finding of misconduct against the Appellant where the evidence relied upon consisted primarily of documentary statements from witnesses who were not called to testify viva voce. The Appellant contends that he successfully refuted the contents of the

statements and that, in the absence of oral testimony, his defence remained unchallenged. He argues that reliance on unverified, uncorroborated statements deprived him of a fair opportunity to challenge the allegations. The Respondent, however, submits that procedural and substantive fairness were observed, that the Appellant was fully aware of the allegations, had an opportunity to respond, and that the documentary evidence relied upon was admissible and sufficient in the circumstances.

In Chataira v ZESA SC 83/01, the Supreme Court held that:


“In a disciplinary hearing against an employee, natural justice requires that the employee should know of the accusations he has to meet, that he should be given an opportunity to state his case and that the internal tribunal acts in good faith. It is not necessary that viva voce evidence be led. The employee must be shown any statements or documentary evidence that is being produced before the disciplinary committee but he need not be afforded all the facilities which are allowed to a litigant in a judicial trial. He need not be given an oral hearing; or allowed representation by an attorney or counsel; he need not be given an opportunity to cross- examine; and he is not entitled to discovery of documents.”

Similarly, in Jerry Musarira v Anglo American Corporation SC 53/05, the Court emphasized that:

“Adequate notice of the hearing must be given; the employee must have an opportunity to be heard and to present his case; and the employee should be allowed to call witnesses if he so wishes.”

The authorities referred have underscored the need for procedural fairness in the conduct of disciplinary proceedings. They have underlined the need to ensure the employee is given notice of the allegations and an adequate opportunity to respond to the allegations, the formalities of cross-examination or viva voce evidence as in a judicial trial are generally regarded as unnecessary unless specifically provided for in the relevant Code of Conduct. In the court’s considered view, the authorities have laid down general guidelines as to how disciplinary proceedings have to be conducted. It is important to note that whilst disciplinary committees are not bound by strict rules of evidence each case has to however be considered on its own facts. Where there are material disputes of facts that cannot be resolved through the documentary evidence alone it becomes necessary for the committee to call for viva voce evidence and allowing cross examination of the witnesses.

In the present case, the Appellant was aware of the allegations and afforded an opportunity to present his defence. He denied all charges, regarding his involvement in four separate transactions involving electricity tokens. The documentary evidence was disclosed to him, and he was able to challenge it. Procedurally, there was no denial of natural justice. Nevertheless, it is a fundamental principle that for a disciplinary decision to stand, the evidence must be sufficient to prove the allegations on a balance of probabilities. In this instance, the evidence consisted primarily of written statements from in instances, unnamed or unverified witnesses, in some instances there was no corroborating testimony adduced. The Appellant in presenting his defence challenged the evidence in the form of witness statements. In the hearing itself he rebutted the allegations as made by the Complainant, he also specifically requested for the witnesses to be called. The Complainant in response indicate in certain instances that the witness was not available and in one instance the witness was said to be unprepared to come and give oral evidence. The record also shows that a witness did appear to give evidence but as it turned out he had not written a prior report, he was deemed improperly before the Disciplinary Committee. The long and short of it is that the Appellant having raised material dispute of facts the truth could only be ascertained by allowing viva voce evidence to be led from the authors of the statements. In the absence of such evidence the documentary statements remained untested before the Disciplinary Committee, the link between the Appellant and the alleged transactions therefore remained unsubstantiated.

A closer examination of the alleged transactions shows that in the first transaction, the client allegedly involved was unnamed, no testimony was obtained from the client, and no proof was provided that the Appellant received any money. In the second transaction, concerning Richman Gwati, the Appellant denied involvement, the client was not called, and no token number was produced. In the third transaction involving Ratidzai Sakala, no witness testified to corroborate the allegation, no financial or transactional record was produced, and the Appellant’s denial of the allegation went unchallenged. In the fourth transaction, concerning Nicholas Chingosho and another unnamed individual, no witnesses were called, and no evidence linked the Appellant to the alleged transaction. Furthermore, the alleged loss of USD$24 000.00 and RTGS1 200,000.00 was not substantiated through evidence.

The Respondent however submits that the evidence was credible and sufficient basing on Bowers on Employment Law (p.217) citing Khanum v MidGlamorgan Area Health Authority [1978] IRLR 215 wherein it was emphasized that procedural fairness in internal disciplinary proceedings requires that:

“the employee must be made aware of the accusations they are required to answer; the employee must be given a reasonable opportunity to state their case; and the internal tribunal must act in good faith.”

It is trite that in civil or disciplinary proceedings the standard of proof is on a balance of probabilities. As stated in ZESA v Dera 1998 (1) ZLR 500 (S), McNally JA noted:

“It is a startling, and in my view, an entirely novel proposition, that in a civil case the standard of proof should be anything other than proof on a balance of probabilities…Moreover, the parties in a civil dispute are equally interested parties, in the sense that each one seeks relief. A claims money from B, B claims an order that he owes nothing; A wishes to dismiss B, B wishes to remain employed…Hence the ‘balance of probability’ test.”

Similarly, in British American Tobacco Zimbabwe v Chibaya SC 30/19, the Court held that:


“It must carry a reasonable degree of probability but not so high as is required in a criminal case. If the evidence is such that the tribunal can say ‘we think it more probable than not’, the burden is discharged, but if the probabilities are equal, it is not.”

While the Appellant was indeed afforded procedural fairness, the court considers that the reliance solely on unverified statements, some of which were anonymous, raises an issue as to whether the Respondent discharged the burden on it to prove the allegations on a balance of probabilities. In this context, fairness and justice require that evidence relied upon to substantiate misconduct be adduced in a form capable of testing its truthfulness. In Mauya v MIPF HH-504-18, the High Court held that procedural fairness may demand an opportunity to confront adverse evidence, particularly when credibility is central to the allegations. In the present case, most if not all allegations were disputed, the documentary evidence remained untested at the end of proceedings. It was in realising the gaps in the evidence that the Appeals Committee itself decided to recall the Complainant to give further evidence before that Committee. Her evidence, as the record will show. Was however focussed on the concept of third party vending, the Complainant was not requested to address the gaps in the documentary evidence tendered through her, which gaps have been high-lighted above. The Appeals Committe did not itself see the need to also call for viva voce evidence from the authors of the statements.

In light of the foregoing, the court finds that the Appeals Committee grossly erred and misdirected itself in upholding the Disciplinary Committee’s determination on conviction and on penalty. The Appeals Committee ought to have found that it was improper for the

Disciplinary Committee to have relied on the witness statements where the statements had been clearly challenged. The circumstances in this case revealed the need for oral testimony in order to ascertain the veracity of the statements. In this regard the appellant’s grounds of appeal have merit and must succeed.

Accordingly, the court makes the following order:


  1. The appeal is allowed with no order as to costs.


  1. The decisions of the Appeals Committee and Disciplinary Committees be and are hereby set aside.


  2. The Appellant is reinstated to his former position with effect from the date of unlawful termination and without any loss of salary and benefits. If reinstatement is no longer tenable, he shall be paid damages in lieu of reinstatement, the quantum of which is to be agreed upon, failing which either party may approach this court for quantification of such damages.
