For Respondent Mr. G.T. Mharapara

MURASI J.,

INTRODUCTION

This application is styled “Application for Condonation of the late Application for Variation of a Court Order.”

Applicant was in the Respondent’s employ. Her employment was terminated and she approached the Labour Officer for relief. The determination of the Labour Officer did not find favour with the Applicant who applied for review of the Labour Officer’s determination in Case Number LC/H/Rev/93/18. This Court, in an Order dated 31 July 2019, set aside the Labour Officer’s determination and ordered that Applicant be paid the sum o $20 456-25. Respondent proceeded to pay the requisite sum in local currency. Again, the Applicant was disgruntled and wrote several letters to the Registrar of this Court. Applicant later decided to apply for the variation of the Court Order. Hence the present application.





PRELIMINARY ISSUES

Mr. Danana, for the Respondent, stated that Respondent discovered that it had not filed heads of argument as required in terms of the Rules of Court. Respondent had therefore made an application for condonation of the late filing of those heads of argument and upliftment of the bar operating against it. He stated that the application was Case Number LC/H/1300/25. He requested that the present matter be postponed to enable the application for condonation to be dealt with.

In response, Mr. Masomera stated that initially Applicant had opposed the granting of the application. However, Applicant was no longer opposing the application to enable the matter to be dealt with on its merits.

The Court proceeded to grant the Order and uplifted the bar operating against the Respondent. The Court also informed Respondent’s legal practitioners that, as they had not brought a hard copy of the heads of argument, they would make oral submissions, and they were agreeable to that position.

MERITS

APPLICANT’S SUBMISSIONS

Mr. Masomera prefaced his submissions with the statement that Applicant had made a request for the consolidation of the present matter with LC/H/Rev/93/18 but had been informed by the Registrar that this could only be done pursuant to an Order by a Judge/Court. The Court stated that reference to that record would be made if absolutely necessary for the judicious determination of the matter. If no issues arose needing a re-visit to that record, then there would be no need to do so.

In submissions, Mr. Masomera stated that Respondent paid the Applicant an amount of $24 456-25 in local currency when Applicant was entitled to be paid the sum of USD$20 456-25. He further stated that Respondent had used the 1:1 parity rate when the Applicant should have been paid according to the prevailing rate of exchange on the date of payment. Asked by the Court how much in United States Dollars had Applicant been paid, he stated that this would amount to about USD$2000-00 as he was not sure about the correct amount. He submitted that Applicant acknowledged that a payment was made.

Mr. Masomera submitted that the factors to be taken into account in considering an application of this nature were listed at page 77 of the record which formed part of the Applicant’s heads of argument. He further stated that it was admitted that the delay was inordinate in the circumstances having regard to the fact that the Court Order was granted in 2019 and the present application was filed in 2025, leading to a delay of six (6) years. Mr. Masomera submitted that on the face of it, it would appear that Applicant was no longer interested in pursuing the matter. He stated that, however, when one has regard to the correspondence filed between 2019 and 2025, the picture shows that Applicant had always intended to pursue the matter. Mr. Masomera added that it was because of the clarity in the matter which had been requested by the Applicant which would then enable the Applicant to be certain of the cause of action and the possible action she was to pursue. He stated that the requested explanation did not come.

As far as prospects of success were concerned, Mr. Masomera submitted that there was clearly a difference in the amount paid by the Respondent and what was due to Applicant. He stated the figure owed to the Applicant to be approximately USD$18 000-00 as he had only accessed the Reserve Bank of Zimbabwe rates after filing the present application. He stated that he also referred to the judgment in Ingalulu v NRZ at page 81 of the record which case also referred to Zambezi Gas v N.R. Barber in paragraph 4 thereof. Mr. Masomera added that the Court Order only had a $ sign with no currency stated and the order should have been in a specific currency. He further argued that it was always going to be difficult to implement the Order granted by the Court.

In addressing the issue of prescription, Mr. Masomera stated that this was an issue raised by the Respondent at page 84 of the record. He referred to the case of Manjovha v Delta which he stated supported his stance on the issue of prescription. He stated that Applicant needed clarification as to what currency was going to be utilised in the payment. He further stated that the application was convenient to both the Court and the Respondent because it would permit closure and save unnecessary expenses by both parties.

On page 67 of the record, Applicant cites the case of Stanley Chidziva v Spar Eastern Region LC/H/417/07 where the Court relied on section 2A of the Labour Act in stating the point that the Court is not supposed to follow rigid formalism and should advance social justice and democracy at the workplace. Reliance was also had to Ingalulu Investments (Pvt) Ltd & Anor v NRZ and Anor SC 42/22 to the point that the payment made by the Respondent was supposed to be based on the rate of exchange subsisting on the date of payment. On the issue of prescription, Applicant’s representative, in the heads of argument, relied on the case of Hodgson v Granger & Anor which was cited in Manjovha v Delta Beverages (Pvt) Ltd SC 64/21.

RESPONDENT’S SUBMISSIONS

As observed earlier in this judgment, the Court stated that the Respondent’s legal practitioners were to make oral submissions as they did not avail a printed copy of the heads of argument. The legal practitioners advised the Court, they had apportioned themselves areas in which each would make submissions.

Mr. Mharapara submitted that the matter was prescribed. He stated that the law was clear on this point. He further submitted that the debt was formed and known at the time Respondent made the payment to the Applicant, which payment was accepted and appropriated. He added that no action was taken by the Applicant to vindicate her rights. He argued that the letters written to the Registrar on Applicant’s behalf did not amount to an application, appeal or review which had the effect of arresting the running of prescription. Mr. Mharapara further stated that the clock started ticking the moment Applicant accepted the payment from the Respondent and the latter had discharged its obligations. He argued that the same remedy which Applicant was now seeking from the court existed then. The cause of action arose from the moment payment was made by the Respondent.

Mr. Mharapara submitted that the present application was coming some six (6) years after the event and as such the delay could not be described as reasonable in the circumstances. He argued that the current process prejudiced the Respondent coming 6 years after because it required a re-visit to its books and identify personnel to ascertain whether the claim was worth attending to. Mr. Mharapara further submitted that Applicant was not sure of the exact amount she is owed by the Respondent as the figures are not expressed anywhere in the present application. He also argued that with the promulgation of Statutory Instrument 33 of 2019, the law provided that any such payments were to be made in local currency and the Court could not, in July of the same year, have pronounced a judgment in foreign currency without an alternative of payment in local currency.

Mr. Danana submitted that the Applicant, both in the Founding Affidavit and in oral submissions, had conceded that the delay was inordinate. He stated that Applicant had stated that she was not sure on how to proceed yet all processes needed to approach the Court were to be done within 21 days. Reliance was made to Lunat v Patel SC 47/22 as to the principle of reasonableness of the application. Mr. Danana further submitted that Applicant received and accepted the payment and therefore waived her right to challenge anything connected with such payment. It was pointed out that Applicant intended to challenge aspects of the payment some 6 years later. He added that the Court could not have granted an Order which contravened the law as the statute required that any orders for payment should be expressed in local currency.

ANALYSIS

THE DELAY

In P.E. Bosman Transport Works Committee & Ors v Piet Bosman Transport (Pty) Ltd 1980 (4) SA 794 (A) it was held by MULLER JA as follows:

“In a case such as the present, where there has been a flagrant breach of the Rules this Court in more than one respect, and where in addition there is no acceptable explanation for some periods of delay and , indeed, in respect of other periods of delay, no explanation at all, the application should , in my opinion, not be granted whatever the prospects of success may be.”

In Ndebele v Ncube 1992 (1) ZLR 288 (S), McNALLY JA made the following observations:

“It is the policy of the law that there should be finality in litigation. On the other hand, one does not want to do injustice to litigants. But it must be observed that in recent years, applications for rescission, for condonation, for leave to apply or appeal out of time, and for other relief arising out of delays either by the individual or his lawyer have rocketed in numbers. We are bombarded with excuses for failure to act. We are beginning to hear more appeals for charity than for justice. Incompetence is becoming a growth industry. Petty disputes are argued and then re-argued until the costs far exceed the capital amount in dispute. The time has come to remind the legal profession of the old adage, vigilantibus non dormientibus jura subveniunt- roughly translated, the law will help the vigilant but not the sluggard.”

Applicant’s founding affidavit makes the concession that the period of delay is inordinate (paragraph 8). In Paragraph 9, the Applicant avers that there were ‘two conflicting theories’. It is not stated who formulated the two ‘conflicting theories., whether it was the Applicant herself or her representative. It is stated in the following paragraph that it became necessary to write to the Registrar and seek clarification and this took place up to 2025. The explanation in the Founding Affidavit is inconsistent with the letter dated 5 June 2020 addressed to the Respondent. The Court will reproduce it as it makes the following averments:

“2. Reference is also made to the self-explanatory letter I wrote to the Registrar of the Labour court dated 25 February 2020.

3. In light of the abovementioned letter I wish to propose a meeting wherein we can discuss a mutual resolution to the outstanding issue of correction and conversion of the said order since we did not receive a written response from the Registrar.

4. Secondly I hope you are already aware of the Zambezi Gas judgment and the CABS/RBZ High Court judgment which I both believe will have a bearing on the issues.

5. It appears to me that if the matter is not resolved by mutual consent of the parties it may require extensive and costly litigation on both sides hence my overtures.

6. I sincerely hope you can agree to a ‘without prejudice’ meeting which we can use to try and resolve all outstanding issues.”

The above letter makes reference to the communication made to the Registrar in February of that year. The June letter proposed a meeting to ‘resolve’ the issue. It also makes indications of pending litigation if the issue is unresolved. This clearly shows that as at June 2020 a decision had been made to engage the Respondent because no reasons were forthcoming from the Registrar of the Labour Court. The reason for the delay cannot therefore be the avalanche of letters written to the Registrar. In Stevenson v Minister of Local Government & Others 2002 (1) ZLR 498 (S), it was held that:

“And, as has been said repeatedly, an Applicant must stand or fall by his founding affidavit and facts alleged in it.”

The averments made in the Founding Affidavit that the reason for delay was not getting responses from the Registrar cannot be correct. The June 2020 letter speaks otherwise. In that letter it is confirmed that no response had been received from the Registrar which was the reason Applicant’s representative was communicating with the Respondent. The Court finds the explanation tendered to be unreasonable and thus unacceptable.

PROSPECTS OF SUCCESS

The Court is of the view that the issue of prescription raised by the Respondent is dispositive of the matter. In Chirinda v Van Der Merwe & Anor HH 51/13, it was held as follows:

“It is therefore trite that prescription runs from the date that a debt becomes due. A debt becomes due when the creditor becomes aware of the identity of the debtor and the facts giving rise to the cause of action. The cause of action in any action or claim is ‘the entire set of facts which give rise to an enforceable claim and includes every act which is material to be proved to entitle a plaintiff to succeed in his claim’”

In John Conradie Trust v The Federation of Kushanda Pre-Schools Trust & Others SC 12/17, BHUNU JA had this to say:

“Prescription does not deal with the merits. It simply seeks to extinguish old stale debts not claimed within the prescribed time limits. The rational for prescription was amply captured by the learned trial judge where he quotes Wessels in The Law of Contracts in South Africa Vol II para 2766 where the learned author says:

‘Creditors should not be allowed to permit claims to grow stale because thereby they embarrass the debtor in his proof of payment and because it is upsetting to social order that the financial relations of the debtor towards third parties should suddenly be disturbed by the demanding from him payment of forgotten claims.’”

Did the letters written by the Applicant to the Registrar of the Labour Court have the effect of arresting the running of prescription. The legal principles relating to the running of prescription militate against finding in favour of the Applicant. In Mukahlera v Clerk of Parliament & Ors 2005 (2) ZLR 365 (HC), PATEL J (as he then was) had this to say:

“It is abundantly clear to me that a letter of demand, whether issued through a lawyer or otherwise, does not constitute ‘process’ in its ordinary sense not within the meaning of that term as defined in section 19 (1) of the Act. Indeed, this was precisely the position taken by SMITH J in Masara & Ors v Forestry Commission & Anor 1999 (1) ZLR 174 (H) at 176, where it was held that a letter of demand by legal practitioners claiming damages, which was followed by negotiations and correspondence to reach a settlement, was not court process and did not operate to interrupt prescription.”

The Applicant’s sojourns between the Registrar and the Respondent, when time was ticking, are not recognised in law as arresters of the running of prescription. Mr. Masomera submitted that he found that the decision by BHUNU JA in Patrick Manjovha v Delta Beverages (Pvt) Ltd SC 64/21 supported his cause. To the contrary, The Learned Judge at page 5 begins by stating that the definition of a debt includes an appeal within the context of the Prescription Act. On the same page it was stated that:

“Subsection (3) (b) however provides that if one fails to successfully prosecute his cause of action and in this case his appeal to finality the interruption shall lapse and the running of prescription shall not be deemed to have been interrupted.”

At page 6:

“As the appellant was eminently aware of his cause of action right from the beginning back in January 2015, his dilatoriness in prosecuting his appeal deserves censure to give effect to the purpose of the Act.”

Applicant’s cause of action arose on the date she received and accepted payment which was in 2019. The letters to the Respondent confirm this position. Evidently, the cause of action arose in 2019, and Applicant is approaching the Court with the intention of resurrecting a prescribed debt in terms of the law. The Court cannot grant such application.

As stated in the John Conradie Case supra, prescription does not deal with the merits, it simply seeks to extinguish old stale debts not claimed within the prescribed time. This therefore means that points raised by Mr. Masomera as regards the prospects of success in the matter do not require the attention of the Court in the circumstances. This is a trite position that when a court finds a point that is dispositive of the matter, it must end there. In Gospel of God International 1932 v Mungweru & Ors SC 99/19 MAKARAU JA (as she then was) noted that:

“The above position is derived from the settled practice of the courts in this jurisdiction. It is settled practice when writing a judgment to decide no more than what is absolutely necessary for the resolution of the legal dispute before the court. (See Nzara and Others v Kashumba N.O. and Others SC 18/18 and the authorities cited therein with approval). Thus, for instance, if the court has no jurisdiction, no matter how interesting the legal point being raised by the parties, the settled practice is for the court to merely decline jurisdiction and withhold expressing an opinion on the interesting legal points. Similarly with findings such as in casu, that the application is ill founded, the court has no basis for proceeding any further. A finding on a dispositive issue should mark the end of the court’s enquiry and of its curiosity too.”

As stated elsewhere in this judgment, the Court formulated the view that the issue of prescription was dispositive of the matter and the Court has, through the cited judgments determined that the Applicant’s cause of action is indeed afflicted with prescription. No further attention is thus required in the matter. An award of costs is appropriate in the circumstances.









The following Order is thus appropriate.

  1. The application for condonation of the late filing of an application for variation of a Court Order is hereby dismissed.

  2. Applicant to meet Respondent’s costs on the ordinary scale
